# Use your Chinese, but don't overrate it

**Yes, use the method, with changes.** The core of it is sound: say sentences aloud from memory and review them on a schedule. The hype is "conversational in 6 weeks" and "skip grammar." Your Chinese helps more than most learners get, but only in some places. It makes Korean nouns, numbers and signs much easier. It does nothing for verb endings and particles, and those are exactly where Chinese speakers make mistakes. So keep a small grammar kit. Use **Traditional Chinese as the default prompt language**, switch to English for skate talk and any sentence you'd naturally think in English, and add Hanja notes on the back of each card. In one month, plan on **about 100–200 practiced sentences and short, scripted skatepark chats**, not free conversation. Tonight's step is AnkiDroid plus an AI-written list of sentences. Build your own app after the trip.

## The drills are proven; the six-week promise is not

Saying the answer from memory and spacing out reviews were the only two techniques rated "high utility" in a major review of study methods ([Dunlosky et al.](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)). Shadowing (repeating right after the audio) is a useful extra for hearing sounds. Listening on repeat is weak on its own. The US Foreign Service Institute estimates **about 2,200 class hours** for English speakers to reach professional Korean ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/nation/2023/11/113_240261.html)). No one has published a matching estimate for Chinese speakers, and we found no data showing that Chinese speakers pass Korean levels faster. "Skip grammar" is wrong for everyone, and especially wrong for you, as the next section explains.

## Chinese gives you nouns, not conversation

Sino-Korean words, the ones built from Chinese characters (Hanja), make up **about 57% of dictionary entries** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary)). In everyday speech, though, a study of recorded university-student conversation found they were **only about 15% of the words people actually said**, while native Korean words were 80% ([KCI](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART001089488)). So the boost lands on nouns like subway, exit, practice and hospital. The common verbs, the endings and the slang stay native Korean. Skate trick names are English written in Hangul (킥플립 = kickflip), so your English helps there more than your Chinese.

The sound patterns are regular enough to help you **recognize** words, though you can't count on them to build new ones. Words ending in -ng in Mandarin end in ㅇ in Korean (公 gōng → 공). Old Chinese -t endings became Korean ㄹ (一 → 일, 鐵 → 철) ([zh.wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hk/%E6%9C%9D%E9%AE%AE%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97%E9%9F%B3)). An initial ㄹ or ㄴ often drops at the start of a word (練習 → 연습) ([Korean spelling rules](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/%EB%8C%80%ED%95%9C%EB%AF%BC%EA%B5%AD_%ED%95%9C%EA%B8%80_%EB%A7%9E%EC%B6%A4%EB%B2%95(%EC%A0%9C2014-39%ED%98%B8))). If you know any Taiwanese (台語), it's an even better guide: 三 sam = 삼, 十 si̍p = 십, 感謝 kám-siā = 감사 ([TVBS](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/970147)). Taiwan usage sometimes matches Korean better than Mainland usage does: 化妝室 ≈ 화장실 (restroom), 醫師 = 의사 (doctor), 藥局 = 약국 (pharmacy). Some words look the same but mean something different. These "false friends" cause the most errors with shared words ([airiti](https://www.airitilibrary.com/Publication/Index/U0021-1610201315293656)):

| Korean | Hanja | You'd guess | It actually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 약속 | 約束 | restrain | plans, an appointment |
| 조심 | 操心 | worry | be careful! |
| 공부 | 工夫 | time, effort | study |
| 신문 | 新聞 | news | newspaper |
| 점심 | 點心 | snack | lunch |
| 편의점 | 便宜店 | cheap store | convenience store |

**Pronunciation priorities change too.** You already make Mandarin's "puffed" and "unpuffed" consonants, so Korean ㅋㅌㅍ (≈ㄎㄊㄆ) and ㄲㄸㅃ (≈ㄍㄉㄅ) come fairly easily. That's the reverse of English speakers' main problem. Chinese learners mostly confuse the *plain* consonants ㄱㄷㅂㅈ with the others ([CityU](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/perception-of-korean-consonants-by-chinese-speakers/)). Your tone ear can fix this. After a plain consonant, the vowel starts low, and after ㅋ or ㄲ it starts high (달 moon, low; 탈 mask, high). Training on this pitch cue helped Chinese learners in one study ([koreascience](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202108458479351.page)). Next, work on the vowels ㅗ and ㅓ, which cause the most vowel errors ([earticle](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A451931)), and on running a final consonant into the next vowel (먹어요 sounds like 머거요). Taiwanese learners tend to pronounce each syllable separately instead ([KoreaScholar](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/412362)). Shadow whole sentences, not single words, so Mandarin tones don't make your Korean sound sing-song. Don't bother telling ㅐ and ㅔ apart, because most young Seoul speakers pronounce them the same. And if you tend to merge -n and -ng in Mandarin, practice pairs like 반 and 방. No study has tested that last point, but it's a likely Taiwan-specific trap.

**Particles are your real gap.** Chinese has no word endings, and studies trace Chinese learners' habit of **dropping particles and verb endings** to their first language ([earticle](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A114780)). One upside: Chinese is built around topics, so 는 ("as for…") feels like 這個嘛… with a pause. Learn the shapes of 은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 에, 에서 and 도. Add five endings: -아요 (present), -았어요 (past), -고 싶어요 (want to), -(으)ㄹ 수 있어요 (can) and -(으)세요 (please do). Make sure every memorized sentence includes them. One more thing works in your favor: Chinese learners tend to be *over*-polite ([Ewha](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/204085)), and that's safe. Use -요 with everyone until a skater suggests speaking casually.

## Prompt in Chinese by default, English where you think in English

Studies of word glosses slightly favor the learner's native language, but the edge mainly shows up on immediate tests ([SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922); [LTR](https://www.todoele.net/bibliografia/relative-effects-l1-and-l2-glosses-l2-learning-meta-analysis)), and one review found no effect ([LTR](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/effect-textual-glosses-l2-vocabulary-acquisition-meta-analysis)). None of them tested speaking from a prompt. The practical rule: **put the prompt in whichever language you'd naturally say that sentence in.** For food, transit and family, that's probably Chinese. For skate talk, apps and tricks, it may be English. Check yourself: whichever prompt brings the Korean to mind faster wins. Both choices work, so set a default (Chinese) and allow changes card by card. On the back, show the Korean, the Hanja for its Sino-Korean nouns only (지하철 地下鐵), and a ⚠ flag on false friends. Don't study Hanja as a separate subject this month. Use it as a memory hook only.

## Roadblocks: Mainland Chinese, mixed politeness, silent audio

**Sentences.** AI models handle Traditional Chinese and Taiwan vocabulary worse than Simplified Chinese ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01858v3)), so your Chinese prompts can slip into Mainland words. In your AI prompt, ask for "Taiwan Traditional Chinese: 捷運 not 地鐵, 影片 not 視頻." Then scan the output for Simplified characters. The Korean has its own known flaw: AI drifts between polite and casual speech, so state who is talking to whom and which politeness level to use. Write numbers out as words, because Korean has two number systems and voices misread digits. Check sentences against Papago, which supports Traditional Chinese ([Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naver_Papago)), and when the AI and Papago disagree, ask a skater: "이거 자연스러워요?" ("Does this sound natural?")

**The app.** Sideloading an APK still works, and installing over USB from your laptop avoids Google's new waiting period ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification)). The hard part is playing audio with the screen off: Android stops background playback unless the app is built carefully ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/audio/)). The quiet risk is losing your data. Keep the master list as a CSV on your laptop and add an export button from day one. Mark text as Korean or as Taiwan Chinese so Hanja display in the right character style.

**Later.** To make the app work "for any learner or niche," use general fields like *native* and *bridge* instead of hard-coding zh and en. Add a field for which language you thought the sentence in, a Hanja field (left empty for languages without one), a false-friend note, and a "checked by" field. Ship sentence sets as "deck packs" (CSV plus audio) and review AI-written sentences before they go live.

## Next step: forty Chinese-prompted cards before you land

Tonight, learn Hangul basics. They take hours, not weeks ([Korean Wiki Project](https://koreanwikiproject.com/wiki/Hangeul_step_1)). Have an AI write about 40 survival and skate sentences into a CSV, with columns for zh-TW, English, Korean and Hanja. Generate the audio on your laptop with Google's free voices ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/pricing?hl=nl)) and import everything into **AnkiDroid**, which is free, offline and records your voice for comparison ([AnkiDroid manual](https://docs.ankidroid.org/)). On day one, learn the Sino-Korean numbers 일 through 십, plus 만 원 (10,000 won), because prices, subway lines and phone numbers all use them. Hongdae has a Chinese–Korean language exchange on Wednesdays ([Airbnb](https://www.airbnb.com.au/experiences/3634744)), and it's a cheap way to get your cards checked.

## Conclusion

Your Chinese speeds up reading signs and remembering nouns. It doesn't help with what really limits you this month: endings, particles and fast casual speech. Treat Hanja as a memory hook, not a shortcut. The skatepark will show which of your sentences work, and the "which language did I think this in" field you add tonight is the feature that makes the future app useful for other learners.

# Keep the drills, drop the six-week hype

**Yes, use the method, but in a changed form.** Its core, saying sentences aloud from memory and reviewing them on a schedule, is one of the best-supported study techniques in learning science. Two of the video's big claims are hype: "conversational in 6 weeks" and "skip grammar." For Korean in one month, expect **about 100–150 practiced sentences and some scripted skatepark chats, not open conversation.** To make it work for Korean, learn the alphabet (Hangul) first, add a very small grammar kit, and keep every sentence in the polite -요 form. Don't build the app before the trip. Tonight, load your first sentences into **AnkiDroid** (free, works offline) and loop the audio in a music player. Build your own app after the trip, using the same sentence file.

## The memory drills are real science; the timeline is not

The best part of the method is step 3: look at the English, say the Korean out loud, check. That is "retrieval practice." In a classic study, people who tested themselves on Swahili words remembered far more a week later than people who re-read them ([Karpicke & Roediger](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2008/06/testing)). A major review of study techniques rated **self-testing and spaced practice as the only two "high utility" methods out of ten** ([Dunlosky et al.](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)), and in language learning, spreading reviews over days has a medium-to-large effect ([Kim & Webb](https://peeref.com/works/24199363)). Learning whole sentences is also smart. Learners do slowly break memorized phrases into reusable grammar, but in a study of school learners that took years, not weeks ([Myles](https://www.essex.ac.uk/-/media/documents/departments/langling/myles-elct-15.pdf)).

The other steps are weaker. Listening on repeat is basically re-reading, which that same review rated "low utility." It gets you used to the sounds but doesn't replace recall. Shadowing (repeating right after the audio) has a few small studies showing it helps you hear sounds more clearly ([Hamada](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380)). That makes it a good add-on, not magic. The "review before sleep" claim comes from a lab study of 16 word pairs. It backs up studying in the evening and reviewing the next morning, but "dramatically" is a stretch ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/sleep-makes-relearning-faster-and-longer-lasting.html)).

The hype is the timeline. The US Foreign Service Institute puts Korean in its hardest group: **about 2,200 class hours** to reach professional level ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/nation/2023/11/113_240261.html)). Six weeks gets you rehearsed lines about your own life. To follow fast, unpredictable replies, you need to know about 95% of the words you hear ([van Zeeland & Schmitt](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)). We found no source for the video's "90% never reach conversation" figure. "Skip grammar" is just wrong: decades of studies find that teaching grammar directly helps adults ([Spada & Tomita](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)). The fair version is "don't make grammar your main activity."

## Korean needs three tweaks before the sentences work

**First, learn Hangul. The basics take hours, not weeks** ([Korean Wiki Project](https://koreanwikiproject.com/wiki/Hangeul_step_1)). Romanized spelling hides how words actually sound: 학교 (school) is pronounced like 학꾜 ([elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/hangul/tensification-preview)). There's a skate bonus too. Trick names are English words written in Hangul (킥플립 = kickflip), so once you can read, you get them for free.

**Second, spend 2–3 hours on a small grammar kit.** The verb goes at the end of the sentence, and small tags called particles (은/는, 이/가, 을/를) show who does what ([Promova](https://promova.com/blog/korean-particles)). Add a few patterns you can reuse: ___ 주세요 (please give me), -고 싶어요 (I want to), -아/어도 돼요? (is it OK if I…?). These turn memorized sentences into fill-in-the-blank templates.

**Third, be polite by default.** Use the -요 form with everyone, including young skaters. Koreans switch to casual speech (반말) only by agreement, often when someone asks 말 놓을까요? ("shall we talk casually?") ([elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/pragmatics/malnoki-banmal-negotiation)). Keep casual versions of a few lines ready for later. Exclamations like 대박 ("awesome!") are fine with anyone.

A realistic month looks like this: about 50 survival sentences (food, transit, prices, numbers, help), about 20 skatepark lines like 같이 타도 돼요? ("Can I skate with you?") and 인스타 있어요? ("Got Instagram?"), and some praise words. Ttukseom's Han River skate area is the largest in Seoul ([Seoul Mediahub](https://mediahub.seoul.go.kr/archives/189162)), but the info online is old, so check Instagram first. Your free time varies, so set a minimum day of 10–15 minutes of reviews and add new sentences only on longer days.

## AI is good at meaning, bad at tone

Top AI models now rank among the best translators ([Slator](https://slator.com/wmt25-preliminary-results-gemini-2-5-pro-gpt-4-1-lead-ai-translation/)), and unlike Papago, you can tell them which politeness level to use. Their known flaw is **sliding between polite and casual speech in the same conversation** ([Korean AI-rater job listing](https://remowork.life/jobs/6ad2c25c6821d16bb339f51c/korean-specialist)). So in your prompt, say who is talking to whom and which level to use throughout. Then ask for the Korean, a literal English gloss, and a note on anything uncertain. Translating back into English won't catch stiff wording, because clunky literal translations often back-translate perfectly ([LangPro](https://www.langpro.com.au/back-translation-the-pros-and-cons/)). Instead, compare with Papago and ask native speakers about any mismatches. The skaters themselves are ideal checkers, and "이거 자연스러워요?" ("Does this sound natural?") makes a good opener.

The audio is almost free: even 3,000 sentences fit inside Google's monthly free allowance for text-to-speech ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/pricing?hl=nl)). Computer voices stumble on numbers, since Korean has two counting systems, so have the AI spell numbers out as words ([Videostew](https://videostew.com/en/blog/26966-ux-details-to-make-all-tts-engines-properly-read-korean)). They also won't teach you casual skater speech, so watch Korean skate videos too.

## The app's hard parts are background audio and lost data

**Sideloading your own APK still works.** Google's new developer checks only apply in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand until they go global in 2027 ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification)). Installing from your laptop over USB is reportedly exempt ([Gadget Hacks](https://android.gadgethacks.com/news/google-android-sideloading-rules-2026-changes-explained/)). The phone-only route has a one-time 24-hour wait ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/google-adds-24-hour-wait-for-unverified.html)).

**Background audio makes or breaks the app.** Android kills audio that plays with the screen off. Expo's docs say playback stops after about 3 minutes without lock-screen controls ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/audio/)), and users report cutoffs at 10–15 minutes even with them ([GitHub](https://github.com/expo/expo/issues/49906)). Web apps are even less reliable. Flutter or native Kotlin can handle it, but they take more setup.

**Lost data is the quiet risk.** Reinstalling with a different signing key wipes the app's data, and browsers can clear a web app's storage. Keep the master list as a CSV on your laptop, name audio files by ID (sk-0001.mp3), and include an export button from day one. Never put an API key inside the app; make the audio on the laptop and ship only the MP3s. Opening the app to any learner or niche later will need a shareable "deck pack" (CSV plus audio) and a review step for new AI sentences. On this trip, though, the real risk is spending your time building instead of studying.

AnkiDroid already does most of what you want. It's free, works offline, schedules reviews, plays audio, imports CSV files, and lets you record yourself and compare ([AnkiDroid manual](https://docs.ankidroid.org/)). Copy the MP3s into a music player on repeat for listening with the screen off. Download Android's offline Korean voice and speech packs, then test it all in airplane mode. Learn Hangul on the plane. Spend your first three days on survival sentences, then add the skate lines and try them out at the park.

## Conclusion

The method's real gap isn't grammar but real Korean input. A sentence list only takes you as far as what you wrote, and the skatepark is where native speech, corrections and motivation all show up together. Treat every session there as a test of your list: keep what worked, fix what got blank looks, and add what you heard. That loop is also the best design brief for the app you build later.

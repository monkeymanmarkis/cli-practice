# Transfer and Interference for Mandarin-L1 (Taiwan) Learners of Korean

Research note, November 2026. Method caveat: `WebFetch` could not reach the main Korean academic hosts in this session (koreascience.kr, scholarworks.bwise.kr, and the NCCU Taiwan repository irtest.lib.nccu.edu.tw all failed DNS resolution because they are not on the session's allowed network list). So every finding below comes from search-engine abstracts and snippets of the papers, not their full texts. Sample sizes and numbers are as the abstracts report them. Almost all studies are of **Mainland Chinese** learners (중국인 학습자). Taiwan-specific studies are marked **[TW]**. Everything else should be treated as Mainland-based and may transfer to Taiwan learners only approximately.

## Pronunciation: what transfers and what interferes

### Takeaway
The Mandarin aspirated/unaspirated contrast is a real advantage with a twist. Korean aspirated ㅋㅌㅍㅊ map well onto Mandarin k/t/p/q, and tense ㄲㄸㅃㅉ map reasonably onto Mandarin unaspirated g/d/b/j. The hard series for Chinese learners is the **plain (lenis) ㄱㄷㅂㅈ**, which gets confused with aspirated sounds at the start of a word and with tense sounds in the middle. That is roughly the reverse of the English-speaker pattern, where tense consonants are the classic problem. The biggest problems for Chinese learners are final consonants (dropping ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ, final ㄹ, mixing up ㄴ/ㅇ), the vowels ㅗ/ㅓ/ㅜ, and tone-driven intonation. A Taiwanese learner who knows some Taiwanese Hokkien (台語) may have an edge with unreleased finals.

### Cited Findings
**Plain / aspirated / tense (평음·격음·경음)**
- Mandarin has only a two-way stop contrast (unaspirated vs aspirated). Korean has a three-way contrast (lax/plain, tense, aspirated). An ERP plus AX-discrimination study of intermediate and advanced Chinese learners identifies this mismatch as the source of difficulty and says the three-way contrast "has been frequently reported as difficult" in earlier studies. — [ICPhS 2019 paper](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_658.pdf)
- A developmental study tracked 10 Chinese learners across three experiments. They picked up **tense consonants relatively easily** but did not separate **lax from aspirated** well, and the author calls for more focus on lax consonants. — [earticle A216528](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A216528); see also [m.earticle A216528](https://m.earticle.net/Article/A216528)
- A VOT-based acoustic study of Chinese learners' stops found that **plain–aspirated confusion was worse than tense–aspirated confusion**. — [koreascience JAKO201415640234323](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201415640234323.pdf) (abstract via search)
- In a perception experiment (16 participants in one study, 28 in a related listening study), learners most often confused **plain with aspirated at the start of a word** and **plain with tense in the middle of a word**. The authors recommend teaching the **pitch of the following vowel** and **closure duration** as cues. — [CityU: Perception of Korean consonants by Chinese speakers](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/perception-of-korean-consonants-by-chinese-speakers/); [koreascience JAKO201929757857389](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201929757857389.pdf)
- Some sources disagree and say Chinese-speaking learners have **particular difficulty with tense consonants**, with tense affricates (ㅉ) singled out. These are secondary or teaching-oriented sources. — [HappyCampus topic wiki](https://www.happycampus.com/aiWrite/topicWiki/305720); [HappyCampus vowel/consonant wiki](https://www.happycampus.com/aiWrite/topicWiki/95579). The developmental and VOT studies above do not support this.
- One teaching summary maps Korean 격음 ≈ Mandarin aspirated (送氣) and Korean 경음 ≈ Mandarin unaspirated (不送氣). — [koreascience JAKO201929757857389](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201929757857389.pdf) (snippet)
- Perception training on the plain–aspirated contrast with 19 Chinese learners noticeably raised their use of **F0 (pitch)** as a cue. The control group's gain was insignificant, and individual results varied a lot. — [koreascience JAKO202108458479351](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202108458479351.page)
- Oh (2021) found that Chinese learners used F0, the main cue in Seoul Korean, in **perception but not yet in production**, which suggests perception comes before production. — [Ewha Pure: Perception–production relation in cue use of Korean stops by native Chinese speakers](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/perception-production-relation-in-the-cue-use-patterns-of-korean--2/)
- Learners' use of F0 and VOT does not reach native-like levels for the three-way contrast. — [Yoon & Idemaru, NoWPhon2 2016](https://blogs.uoregon.edu/nowphon2016/files/2016/05/NoWPhon2_paper_12-1u6y952.pdf)
- **Tensification rules** (경음화, for example 학교 → [학꾜]): a production and perception study of 20 Chinese learners found they remain a problem past the intermediate level, especially at the sentence level. — [Sookmyung ScholarWorks 9015](https://scholarworks.sookmyung.ac.kr/handle/2020.sw.sookmyung/9015)

**Final consonants (받침/종성)**
- A Jeju National University study classified final-consonant errors as **omission, substitution, or addition**. **Omission was most frequent**, and it occurred on the stop finals /ㄱ/, /ㄷ/, /ㅂ/, which Mandarin lacks. — [Jeju Repository 22934](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/22934)
- A comparison of Southern-dialect speakers (whose dialects keep the entering-tone finals [p], [t], [k], 入聲) with Northern-dialect speakers (who lack them) found that uncertainty about a final's representative sound leads to omission. The same study reports **epenthetic-vowel insertion** (매개 모음 첨가) as another error type. — [Jeju Repository 24487](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/24487)
- Learners also **add an extra consonant** in linking contexts after a final ㄴ (they geminate it), and this happens regardless of region or proficiency. — [Jeju Repository 24487](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/24487) / [Jeju 22934](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/22934) (search summary does not say which)
- In Kim Tae-kyung (2014)'s listening experiment, learners had the most trouble identifying the **place of articulation of final stops** (ㄱ vs ㄷ vs ㅂ). — [koreascience JAKO201611962056302](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201611962056302.page?&lang=en)
- A study of 107 Chinese learners says Korean final consonants differ greatly from Mandarin finals. Even the nasal finals ㄴ/ㅇ are only "close, not identical" to Mandarin -n/-ng, which causes confusion. — [Ewha DSpace 252834](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/252834)
- **Final ㄹ** had the **most errors** among finals in one study. Learners also tend to pronounce final ㄹ as a **Mandarin retroflex** (r-coloured, like 兒化). Initial ㄹ as [l] is easy because Mandarin has /l/. — [koreascience JAKO201929757857389](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201929757857389.pdf); [wikidocs 181131](https://wikidocs.net/181131)
- **[TW]** A study of Taiwanese learners found a **strong tendency to pronounce syllable-final consonants** in full, plus frequent errors where they pronounce word by word instead of linking (연음) within a prosodic unit. — [KoreaScholar 412362](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/412362)
- **[TW]** An NCCU master's thesis (2019) argues that Taiwanese learners struggle even after studying phoneme correspondences, because Korean is processed by syllable structure. — [NCCU 128635 (PDF)](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/bitstream/140.119/128635/1/700201.pdf)
- **[TW]** A Taiwanese study of English found that **Taiwanese Hokkien (台語) native speakers produce unreleased syllable-final stops better than Mandarin speakers**. That study is about English, not Korean, but the mechanism (Hokkien -p/-t/-k entering tones) applies directly to Korean ㅂ/ㄷ/ㄱ finals. — [NPUST: 台語入聲到英語塞音](https://sites.google.com/view/dmlnpust/論文項目/語言學/音韻移轉研究台語入聲到英語塞音)

**Vowels**
- In an acoustic study of 10 advanced learners (2024), **ㅗ had the most errors**, followed by ㅓ, ㅜ, ㅣ, ㅐ. **ㅏ and ㅡ had the fewest.** — [earticle A451931](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A451931)
- Typical perception confusions are **ㅗ→ㅜ, ㅗ→ㅓ, ㅓ→ㅗ**. — [KyoboBook 4010069921684: back-vowel perception errors](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010069921684)
- Beginner textbook research names mistaken perception of **ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅓ** as a main error source. — [Ewha DSpace 245975](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/245975)
- ㅡ [ɨ/ɯ] is close in articulation to the Mandarin apical "-i" (as in 資/思), and ㅓ [ə/ʌ] is close in height to Mandarin e [ɤ] (as in 哥). — [wikidocs 181131](https://wikidocs.net/181131). Conflict: some contrastive sources list ㅡ as having no Mandarin counterpart, yet the 2024 acoustic study found it among the easiest. — [earticle A451931](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A451931)

**Tones and intonation**
- Earlier studies explain Chinese learners' Korean intonation errors as **interference from L1 tone**. — [Yune 2010, koreascience JAKO201013351021564](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201013351021564.pdf)
- L1 interference and a universal **compression of pitch range** coexist in intermediate and advanced learners' pitch on Sino-Korean words (2024), but the effect was limited. — [koreascience JAKO202409439606214](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202409439606214.page?&lang=ko); see also [Yune 2021, JAKO202108458471349](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202108458471349.pdf)
- 20 Chinese learners, compared with 10 native speakers, **failed to use accentual-phrase boundaries and tonal patterns** to tell compound nouns from relative clauses. Performance differed by proficiency. — [ERICA ScholarWorks 15526](https://scholarworks.bwise.kr/erica/handle/2021.sw.erica/15526)
- A study comparing 15 Chinese speakers with 6 native speakers looked at whether learners produce Korean accentual-phrase pitch contours, which depend on phrase length and on the initial segment (aspirated or tense initials start high, plain initials start low). — [koreascience JAKO201223659810466](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201223659810466.page)

### Inferences
- **Compared with English speakers:** English speakers' main stop problem is the tense series ㄲㄸㅃ, which English lacks; they tend to hear ㄲ as "g". A Taiwan Mandarin speaker already makes unaspirated [k t p] (ㄍ ㄉ ㄅ) and aspirated [kʰ tʰ pʰ] (ㄎ ㄊ ㄆ). Practical mapping: **ㄲ ≈ ㄍ (g in 哥), ㄸ ≈ ㄉ, ㅃ ≈ ㄅ, ㅉ ≈ ㄐ/ㄗ**; **ㅋ ≈ ㄎ, ㅌ ≈ ㄊ, ㅍ ≈ ㄆ, ㅊ ≈ ㄑ/ㄘ**. Korean tense sounds are also tighter (more laryngeal tension) than Mandarin unaspirated ones, so the match is approximate. What needs deliberate work is **plain ㄱㄷㅂㅈ**: in Seoul Korean they are slightly aspirated at the start of a word, with a **low pitch** on the next vowel, and voiced between vowels. A useful tip for a tone-language speaker: plain consonant → say the vowel low (like a 3rd-tone start); aspirated or tense → say it high (like 1st tone). Example: 달 "moon" (low) vs 탈 "mask" (high) vs 딸 "daughter" (high, tense). This exploits Mandarin pitch sensitivity, and the F0-training study suggests it is trainable.
- Mandarin pitch sensitivity cuts both ways. It helps with the pitch cue for stops, but carrying lexical tones over makes Korean sound "sing-song". Korean intonation is phrase-level (accentual phrases typically rise, roughly L-H or H-H, at phrase ends), not syllable-level. Shadowing whole TTS sentences, not single words, targets this.
- **Finals:** expect the learner to either drop 받침 ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ (Mandarin habit) or, as the Taiwan study suggests, pronounce them too fully and fail to link them into the next vowel (먹어요 should be [머거요], not [먹-어요]). For a beginner, linking (연음) probably matters more for being understood than perfect final release. If the learner speaks any 台語, the 入聲 finals in words like 六 lak, 十 tsa̍p, 一 it are ready-made models for unreleased ㄱ/ㅂ/ㄷ. Fluency in Hokkien is not stated, so this is hypothetical.
- **Not found in sources (general phonetics knowledge, flagged):** many Taiwan Mandarin speakers merge -in/-ing and -en/-eng and often front retroflexes (zh→z, sh→s). The -n/-ng merger could make 반 (ban) vs 방 (bang) harder to hear and say. This is a plausible Taiwan-specific risk with no Korean-learning study behind it. Retroflex fronting probably helps rather than hurts: Korean ㅅ/ㅈ/ㅊ are non-retroflex, so a Taiwanese speaker is less likely than a Beijing speaker to retroflex them. Taiwanese Mandarin is also less r-coloured (less 兒化), which may reduce the final-ㄹ-as-retroflex error, but this is untested.
- **ㄹ:** at the start of a syllable and between vowels, ㄹ is a flap [ɾ] (closer to a quick Mandarin l than to r). In final position it is [l], with the tongue tip touching and no curl. Tell the learner never to use Mandarin 兒 /ɚ/ for final ㄹ.
- **ㅐ/ㅔ:** these are merged for most younger Seoul speakers (general knowledge; no source retrieved in this session), so the learner should not spend effort separating them.
- **ㅓ vs ㅗ:** this is the high-priority vowel pair. Rounded ㅗ is like Mandarin o in 窩 but tighter. Unrounded ㅓ is open, roughly between 哥 e and 阿 a. Mishearing 거 vs 고 matters in daily life (for example 어서 "go ahead/please" vs 오세요 "please come" contexts).

## Grammar: errors and helpful similarities

### Takeaway
The biggest structural gap is SOV order plus particles and verb endings, which an isolating language like Mandarin lacks entirely. Chinese learners' classic errors are particle **substitution** (most common), then **omission**, then addition, plus dropping verb endings. Real positive transfer comes from topic-prominence and null subjects (both languages drop subjects freely), counters that correspond roughly one-to-one with Chinese 量詞, and Sino-Korean vocabulary.

### Cited Findings
- Hak (2020, 한국어문화교육): Chinese learners' particle error types rank **substitution > omission > addition**. Causes are L1 Chinese transfer, overgeneralization of rules, avoidance strategies, and learning environment. By level, 이/가 error rates ran advanced > intermediate > beginner and 을/를 ran beginner > advanced > intermediate. — [KyoboBook 4010069717249](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010069717249)
- An Ewha experimental thesis found that substitution errors in subject and object particles **did not differ by proficiency**, which conflicts in part with Hak (2020). — [Ewha DSpace 213139](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/213139?mode=full)
- Kim Sun-jung & Kim Seong-su (2009) analysed compositions and found **omission of particles and endings** caused by Chinese interference, plus errors from overgeneralization and simplification. — [earticle A114780](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A114780)
- A Sungshin thesis analyses omission, addition and substitution separately for 이/가, 을/를, 은/는, and finds that advanced learners' particle omission looks **intentional** (strategic, as natives do in speech). — [Sungshin Repository 6134](https://repository.sungshin.ac.kr/handle/2025.oak/6134)
- An analysis of 1,168 sentence-ending errors by Chinese-speaking learners in the NIKL learner error corpus found mostly **form errors** in writing and **pronunciation errors** in speech, with relatively frequent **honorific-level substitution** in speech. — [KyoboBook 4010028917608](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010028917608)
- **Topic-prominence:** Korean is classed as having features of **both subject-prominent and topic-prominent** languages, while Chinese is topic-prominent. Contrastive studies match Korean double-subject and topicalized sentences to Chinese 主謂謂語句 (for example 大象鼻子長 ≈ 코끼리는 코가 길다 "elephants have long noses"). — [Inha Korean studies PDF](https://korea.inha.ac.kr/sites/inhakorea/upfiles/tb_kor_study/76/9.pdf); [Ewha DSpace 212440](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/212440)
- **Counters:** a corpus comparison of Korean 분류사 and Chinese 量詞 (class, measure, and action counters) found they **broadly correspond**, though some Korean counters correspond to Chinese nouns or to nothing. — [Jeju Repository 23681](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/23681); Yu Jeongjeong (2014) says comparing classifiers "will be very helpful for Chinese students" — [earticle A220518](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A220518)

### Inferences
- **은/는 vs 이/가:** Mandarin marks topic by word order and pause (這本書我看過), not by a particle. So the learner already has the topic *concept*, which English speakers lack, but needs a reflex to attach 는. A practical rule for memorized sentences: 저는 … ("as for me") to introduce yourself or contrast; 이/가 for new information and in question words or answers (뭐가 맛있어요? "what's good here?", 이게 좋아요 "this one's good").
- **Counters:** the concept transfers directly (개 ≈ 個, 명 ≈ 名/位, 잔 ≈ 杯, 번 ≈ 次/遍, 권 ≈ 本, 장 ≈ 張, 대 ≈ 台). Several are literally the same Hanja: 名→명, 杯→배 (in 건배 "cheers"), 張→장, 臺→대, 卷→권. Two traps (general grammar knowledge, consistent with the Jeju finding of partial non-correspondence): (1) order is **noun + number + counter**: 커피 두 잔 "two coffees", not 두 잔 커피 the way 兩杯咖啡 would suggest. (2) Most everyday counters take **native Korean numbers** (하나/한, 둘/두, 셋/세, 넷/네), not Sino 일/이/삼. 개, 명, 잔, 번, 시 (hour) are native-number counters. 분 (minutes), 원 (won), and 층 (floor) take Sino numbers.
- **Null subjects:** both languages drop subjects (and Korean drops objects) freely, so the learner will not overuse 나/너 the way English speakers often overuse 저는 / 당신. This is an advantage. 당신 should be avoided entirely as "you".
- **Verb endings:** Mandarin has aspect particles (了/過/著) but no inflection. Tense and politeness endings (-아요/-어요, past -았어요/-었어요, -고 싶어요 "want to", -(으)ㄹ 수 있어요 "can") are the main things to learn, and the documented error is *omitting* them. Mandarin 了 also maps poorly onto Korean past tense (了 is perfective, not past), a well-known teaching point. No Korean-learner source on 了 was retrieved in this session.
- **SOV:** this is the same gap for English and Mandarin speakers, so it gives no relative advantage either way. Chinese learners do get one small bonus: modifiers go before the noun in both languages (我昨天買的書 ≈ 내가 어제 산 책 "the book I bought yesterday"). English puts relative clauses after the noun.

### Gaps
- No frequency data for 은/는 errors specifically was retrieved.
- No study comparing Chinese and English learners' grammar error rates head-to-head was found.

## Honorifics and culture for a Taiwanese learner in Korea

### Takeaway
Research on Chinese learners shows a tendency toward **over-politeness**: they use honorific forms regardless of the relationship, and they switch speech levels inappropriately. For a one-month visitor, defaulting to 해요체 is safe. With skaters, the risk is not rudeness but sounding stiff, and the social switch to 반말 is negotiated by age.

### Cited Findings
- In request speech acts, Chinese learners of Korean **mostly used honorific expressions regardless of the relationship with the listener**, which produced inappropriate over-politeness. — [Ewha DSpace 204085](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/204085)
- Intermediate academic learners switching between 합쇼체 and 해요체 in presentations frequently used **inappropriate speech levels**, and their awareness did not match their actual use. — [CAU ScholarWorks 42323](https://scholarworks.bwise.kr/cau/handle/2019.sw.cau/42323)
- Ability to produce and understand honorifics improves with proficiency across social variables. — [Hansung DSpace 9311](https://dspace.hansung.ac.kr/handle/2024.oak/9311)
- Spoken-corpus errors by Chinese-speaking learners include relatively frequent substitution of honorific level. — [KyoboBook 4010028917608](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010028917608)
- Taiwanese media report that many Taiwanese are surprised, even put off, when Koreans ask their age at first meeting. In Korea, even a one-year age gap means using 언니/오빠/형/누나 and honorifics, and only people of the same age are easily treated as equal friends. — [udn 9253701](https://udn.com/news/story/6810/9253701)
- Switching to 반말 requires **mutual confirmation**, and using it before that is seen as offensive. Korean YouTubers living in Taiwan describe Taiwan's "teachers and students as equals" and "friends across ages" norms as culture shocks, which implies Taiwan is notably looser. — [TVBS 1707511](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/1707511)
- Koreans ask age early to establish senior/junior relationships, and speech style changes once the relationship becomes closer. — [Talk To Me In Korean blog](https://blog.talktomeinkorean.com/why-do-koreans-ask-how-old-are-you-so-early/)

### Inferences
- Taiwan's seniority culture (學長/學姊, 前輩) gives the learner the right *instinct*. The new part is that Korean encodes this in every verb ending, and the norm is stricter than Taiwan's for small age gaps. Useful memorized lines: 몇 살이에요? / 몇 년생이에요? ("how old are you? / what year were you born?"), 말 편하게 해도 돼요? ("can we speak casually?"), 말 놓으세요 ("feel free to drop the formality", said to someone younger).
- With skaters, likely in their teens to twenties, expect quick moves to 반말 among peers. The learner should **understand** 반말 (dropping -요: 가자 "let's go", 대박 "awesome", 잘한다 "nice one") but **produce** 해요체 until invited. Over-politeness is the documented Chinese-learner error, and it is socially low-risk.
- 반말 with no prior agreement is a bigger faux pas than any pronunciation error. This matches the culture-shock reports, though no source quantifies it.
- Sources on skate-scene etiquette in Korea specifically were not searched in this scope.

### Gaps
- No studies specifically on Taiwanese (as opposed to Mainland) learners' honorific use were found.
- No sources on Korean skateboarding subculture's speech norms were found.

## Timeline: do Chinese speakers progress faster, and what is realistic?

### Takeaway
No hard data was found that separates hours-to-TOPIK for Chinese and English speakers. No placement or progression statistics from Yonsei, Sogang, or SNU institutes were found either. The consensus advantage is **vocabulary** (Sino-Korean is about 57% of dictionary entries), which mostly pays off at intermediate-and-above levels and in reading, not in the first month of street conversation.

### Cited Findings
- FSI rates Korean as a Category IV ("super-hard") language at about **2,200 class hours** for English speakers to reach professional proficiency. — [Migaku: How long does it take to learn Korean](https://migaku.com/blog/korean/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-korean); [All Language Resources](https://www.alllanguageresources.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-korean/)
- A Japanese-language guide cites FSI Category IV and gives a rough cumulative estimate of about 1,200–1,700 hours for TOPIK 3–4. It is unclear whether this is for English or Japanese speakers, and it is not a primary source. — [Univext blog 394](https://univext.com/ja/blog/394/kankokugo-gakushuu-kikan-naganori)
- Sino-Korean share of vocabulary: estimates range from 30–70%. The NIKL standard dictionary figure is about **57%**. — [Wikipedia: Sino-Korean vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary); a 1957 dictionary study puts it at 53% — [NCCU 54636](https://irtest.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/54636)
- A cross-language overview reports that about **87–99% of Sino-Korean cognates have consistent basic meanings** with Chinese, and that about 11–18% need special attention because their meanings partly differ. — [NINJAL repository 2000520](https://repository.ninjal.ac.jp/records/2000520)
- Positive transfer for same-form synonyms, negative transfer where the Chinese meaning is broader (Taiwanese thesis, Korean→Chinese direction). — [Airiti U0017-1511201111455115](https://www.airitilibrary.com/Article/Detail/U0017-1511201111455115)
- Regular correspondences between Hanja and their Korean readings help Chinese learners read Sino-Korean words. — [ACL Anthology Y18-1050](https://aclweb.org/anthology/Y18-1050.pdf)
- Korean-education research assumes that learners from Chinese-character backgrounds (Chinese, Japanese) can "easily infer the meaning of Sino-Korean words" through character shape or reading, unlike other learners. — [KoreaScholar 270201](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/270201); [KoreaScholar 265366](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/265366)
- Anecdotal: a Korean-studies mailing list post says native Chinese speakers "seem to pick Korean up more quickly than most foreign learners" thanks to shared vocabulary. — [KoreanStudies list, 2003](https://koreanstudies.com/pipermail/koreanstudies_koreanstudies.com/2003-April/003677.html). Forum discussion of Mandarin speakers and conversational Korean — [Straight Dope boards](https://boards.straightdope.com/t/how-hard-is-learning-conversational-korean-for-mandarin-speakers/542178) (anecdotal, old)
- **[TW]** A Dcard learner says the first lesson on vowels and consonants showed Korean is "a different world" (from Japanese). ### Inferences
- **Realistic adjusted estimate (inference, no source):** the FSI 2,200 hours is for professional proficiency (roughly TOPIK 5–6), not TOPIK 2–3. For a Mandarin+English bilingual, the Sino-Korean bonus plausibly cuts intermediate-and-above vocabulary time substantially, perhaps by a quarter to a third of total hours. Phonology (plain consonants, finals) and morphology (particles, endings) get **no shortcut** compared with English speakers. The commonly repeated claim that Chinese speakers "progress faster at institutes" is plausible and widely believed but **unverified**. No placement or progression statistics were found.
- Hanja recognition requires the learner to *hear* 학교 and link it to 學校, or 도서관 to 圖書館. That only works once Korean sound-to-Hanja reading correspondences are known. Examples: 學 hak→학, 國 guó→국, 韓 hán→한 (note how Mandarin -n/-ng and lost -k/-p/-t finals line up with Korean finals, and 台語 often preserves them: 學 ha̍k, 國 kok). Since the learner reads Traditional characters (the same forms as Korean Hanja), the bridge is shorter than for a Simplified-only reader. This is an inference.
- **For one month:** skater and daily-life vocabulary is heavily native Korean or English loans (보드 "board", 킥플립 "kickflip", 대박 "awesome", 진짜 "really", 잘한다 "nice one"), so the Sino-Korean advantage is weaker here than in classrooms or TOPIK. Practical estimate (inference): with roughly 1–2 hours/day of shadowing and recall plus immersion, a Taiwanese beginner can plausibly reach roughly TOPIK 1 functional range. That means reading Hangul fluently, 100–300 rehearsed phrases, understanding prices, numbers and directions with gestures, and being understood in short memorized exchanges. They would not reach TOPIK 2 or follow free conversation. The pronunciation advantage on ㅋ/ㄲ and the Hanja advantage on signs (병원 "hospital", 지하철 "subway", 출구 "exit", 화장실 "toilet") are the concrete month-one wins.

## Should the "skip grammar" advice change for a Chinese speaker?

### Takeaway
Partly. For one month, chunk-based memorized sentences remain right. But because **particle and ending omission is the documented L1-transfer error** for Chinese speakers, and it can fossilize, the memorized sentences should always contain correct particles and endings, and the learner should be taught a handful of them explicitly rather than "skipping grammar" wholesale.

### Cited Findings
- Omission of particles and endings is traced to **Chinese interference**. — [earticle A114780](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A114780)
- Particle substitution and omission persist into advanced levels, and some substitution errors do not decline with proficiency. — [KyoboBook 4010069717249](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010069717249); [Ewha DSpace 213139](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/213139?mode=full)
- Pronunciation difficulties such as tensification persist past the intermediate level, and authors recommend continued explicit focus. — [Sookmyung ScholarWorks 9015](https://scholarworks.sookmyung.ac.kr/handle/2020.sw.sookmyung/9015)
- Perception training on pitch cues measurably helped Chinese learners. — [koreascience JAKO202108458479351](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202108458479351.page)

### Inferences
- **Revised advice for this learner (inference):**
  1. Skip grammar *theory*, but explicitly learn the shapes of about **6 particles** (은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 에 "at/to", 에서 "at (doing)", 도 "also") and **about 5 endings** (-아요/-어요 present, -았어요/-었어요 past, -고 싶어요 "want to", -(으)ㄹ 수 있어요 "can", -(으)세요 "please do"). A Mandarin speaker has no inflectional instinct to fall back on, whereas an English speaker at least expects verbs to change.
  2. Use the topic-prominence intuition: frame 은/는 as "Chinese topic + pause" (這個嘛… ≈ 이건…).
  3. Spend the pronunciation budget on **plain vs aspirated pitch** (low vs high vowel start), **ㅓ vs ㅗ**, and **linking finals into the next vowel**. Spend little on tense consonants (likely easier than for English speakers) or ㅐ/ㅔ (merged).
  4. Use TTS shadowing at the **phrase level** to counter tone transfer.
  5. Build a Hanja bridge list for signs and transit vocabulary, a cheap win using Traditional-character literacy.
- Since natives drop particles in casual speech too, particle omission in short spoken exchanges with skaters is low-cost in month one. The fossilization risk matters only if the learner keeps studying afterward. The advice is "learn them correctly in chunks", not "drill them".

### Gaps
- No studies directly compare explicit-grammar and chunk-only approaches for Chinese-L1 learners of Korean in short immersion.

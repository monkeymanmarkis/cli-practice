## Q1. What share of Korean vocabulary is Sino-Korean: dictionary vs everyday speech vs skate/youth slang?

### Takeaway
About 57% of 표준국어대사전 headwords are Sino-Korean (한자어). In actual conversation, though, Sino-Korean words make up only about **15% of word tokens**, and native words about **80%** (one university-student spoken corpus). The Hanja advantage covers many dictionary *entries* but a much smaller share of *what you hear*. It lands mostly on nouns, while grammar, verbs, adjectives and slang stay native or English-derived.

### Cited Findings
- 표준국어대사전 (Standard Korean Language Dictionary, NIKL): about **290,000 of 510,000 entries (about 57%)** are Sino-Korean — [Korean-to-Chinese MT using Chinese Character as Pivot Clue, arXiv 1911.11008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.11008); same 57% figure in [Wikipedia: Sino-Korean vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary) (snippet only).
- 우리말샘 (NIKL's open dictionary) headwords: **한자어 47.2%, 고유어 22.8%** (remainder loanwords and hybrids) — [국립국어원, 『숫자로 살펴보는 우리말 2022』](https://korean.go.kr/common/download.do;front=77DA44BD2C53E92BC3604821AE8430A1?file_path=etcData&c_file_name=940361cb-62df-4a45-b471-926c5478f3c8.pdf&o_file_name=2022+%EC%88%AB%EC%9E%90%EB%A1%9C+%EC%82%B4%ED%8E%B4%EB%B3%B4%EB%8A%94+%EC%9A%B0%EB%A6%AC%EB%A7%90.pdf). The dictionary share depends on which dictionary you count.
- Higher estimate in a Berkeley linguistics handout reproduced on a forum: about **69% Sino-Korean, 24% native, 6% recent loans**, and "among the most commonly used words, the percentage of Sino-Korean and native Korean words is about the same" — [WordReference forum thread](https://forum.wordreference.com/threads/over-half-of-korean-vocabulary-is-recognizable-as-chinese.450262/) (secondary source, use with caution).
- A TVBS report cites "about 70% of Korean vocabulary" as Hanja-based — [TVBS新聞網](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/970147). Journalistic, no source given.
- **Spoken-corpus evidence (the key number):** 임소영·안주호 (2004, 『한글』) analysed Yonsei's 대학생 구어 전사 말뭉치 (100,017 어절 / 126,507 words) — [KCI record](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART001089488); [DBpia](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE02352645):
  - 고유어 (native): **52.01% of word types, 80.09% of tokens**
  - 한자어 (Sino-Korean): **26.60% of types, 14.97% of tokens**
  - English loanwords: **4.54% of types, 1.40% of tokens**
  - The authors argue that dictionary/written-text ratios need revising for spoken language.
- Elementary-school textbooks: 한자어 **57.12% of types** but only **25.56% of usage frequency**; 고유어 25.28% of types but **64.54% of frequency** — [최운선, 초등 교과서 고유어·한자어 비율](https://www.koredu.net/uploads/papers/ART001689717.pdf).
- Sino-Korean words are mainly literary/technical and abstract vocabulary — [Wikipedia: Sino-Korean vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary) (snippet).
- Comparable Japanese data: a 1995 study of TV shows found only **18% Sino-Xenic vocabulary** in Japanese TV speech — [Wikipedia: Sino-Xenic vocabularies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Xenic_vocabularies) (snippet). Same pattern: much lower in speech.
- NIKL's 2002 new-words report: of 408 new coinages, **204 (50%)** were wholly or partly Sino-Korean — cited in a [search result summary of korean.go.kr materials](https://www.korean.go.kr/nkview/nknews/200307/60_4.html). New coinages are not all slang.
- Skateboarding trick names come from English (ollie, kickflip, heelflip, nollie, 360 flip, boardslide, tailslide) — [Wikipedia: Skateboarding trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skateboarding_trick); [Red Bull skate glossary](https://redbull.com/us-en/skateboarding-glossary). I found no source documenting Korean skate-scene usage directly.

### Inferences
- A realistic planning figure: **about 1 in 7 words heard in casual conversation is Sino-Korean** (15% of tokens). Among *content nouns* (places, objects, activities, numbers) the share is much higher, because particles (은/는, 이/가, 을/를), verb endings (-아요, -고 싶어요) and core verbs/adjectives (가다, 하다, 타다, 좋다, 멋있다) are native and very frequent. Note: the 2004 corpus counts may include particles/endings as words. That would push the native token share up and is part of why native tokens dominate.
- The 2004 corpus is 20+ years old; youth speech since then has probably gained English loans (no data found). 1.4% English tokens likely understates 2026 skatepark talk.
- Expected skatepark vocabulary by origin *[my inference; Korean forms from general knowledge, not sourced]*:
  - **English loans (written in Hangul):** 보드 board, 스케이트보드, 스케이트파크/스케이트장, 올리 ollie, 킥플립 kickflip, 힐플립, 그라인드, 슬라이드, 램프, 레일, 렛지/레지 ledge, 데크 deck, 트럭, 휠, 베어링, 스폰서, 영상(Sino) / 클립. Most tricks are English; a Taiwanese learner's English helps here more than Hanja does.
  - **Native:** 타다 to ride, 넘어지다 to fall, 다치다 to get hurt, 잘하다 to be good at, 멋있다 cool, 쩐다 / 미쳤다 (slang "insane"), 한 번 더 one more time, 같이 together.
  - **Sino-Korean that does show up:** 기술 技術 (trick/skill, as in "무슨 기술이에요?"), 연습 練習 practice, 운동 運動 exercise, 영상 映像 video, 촬영 撮影 filming, 부상 負傷 injury, 위험 危險 dangerous, 조심 操心 careful, 형 兄 older brother (term a male uses for older males, very common between skaters), 선배 先輩 / 후배 後輩, 친구 親舊 friend.
- Youth slang mixes origins. Abbreviations often hide Sino-Korean parts (e.g., 갑분싸 = 갑자기 분위기 싸해짐, with 분위기 雰圍氣), but they are not recognizable by Hanja in real time. *[inference]*

### Gaps

- No source quantifies the share of English loans or Sino-Korean words in skateboarding or youth-slang speech in Korea.

## Q2. Is Taiwan's Traditional script an advantage over Mainland Simplified readers?

### Takeaway
Yes in principle: Korean Hanja are traditional forms, so 學, 電, 鐵, 號, 驛 look exactly as a Taiwanese reader knows them. In practice the advantage is **small**. Modern Korean daily life almost never shows Hanja (signs, menus and chats are Hangul), so the script match helps mainly when looking up a word's Hanja in a dictionary or on flashcards. No empirical study comparing Taiwanese vs Mainland learners turned up. A second, possibly larger edge is **Taiwan-specific vocabulary** that happens to match Korean usage.

### Cited Findings
- Mainland China and Singapore use simplified characters; Taiwan and Hong Kong use traditional — [speakable.co.kr blog](https://speakable.co.kr/blog/chinese-simplified-traditional).
- A HelloTalk user (Mainland learner) reports that after learning dozens of Hanja for Korean, traditional characters felt more convenient. Anecdote only — [HelloTalk post](https://www.hellotalk.com/m/IbW5qyvIJKVkZD).
- South Korea's *Basic Hanja for Educational Use* list has 1,800 characters (traditional forms) — [Wikipedia: Basic Hanja for Educational Use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Hanja_for_Educational_Use); [Wikibooks: Korean/Getting started on Hanja](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Korean/Getting_started_on_Hanja). Wikibooks adds that Hanja are "not necessary" for beginners but help vocabulary growth.
- I found **no peer-reviewed study** comparing Traditional-literate (Taiwan/HK) and Simplified-literate (Mainland) learners of Korean. Search of KCI/RISS-indexed titles turned up only Mainland-focused work (e.g., [尹璐, 중국 내 한국어 학습자를 위한 한자어 교육 연구](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/detail?nodeId=T13851412), which selects 197 basic Sino-Korean words for learners in China) and one Taiwan-specific study of learners' vocabulary strategies (왕청동 2014, 『언어와 정보사회』 23, cited in a reference list; content not verified).

### Inferences
- The script advantage is real but marginal. Educated Mainland readers usually recognize traditional forms with some effort, and Hangul is the only script the learner needs to *use*. Where traditional literacy pays off: (a) reading Hanja on Naver dictionary entries and in Hanja-annotated decks; (b) occasional Hanja in newspaper headlines, signs for temples/palaces, name cards, restaurant names; (c) possibly Seoul subway station signs, which I believe show Hanja names for many stations, though I could not verify this.
- Small glyph differences exist between Taiwan's MOE standard forms and Korean/Kangxi forms (e.g., Korean 寫眞 uses 眞 where Taiwan writes 真). These are trivial for recognition. *[linguistic knowledge, unsourced]*
- **Taiwan-vocabulary matches** (likely more useful than script) *[my inference; Korean forms are standard, Taiwan/Mainland usage from general knowledge]*:
  - 醫師 (TW) = 의사 醫師 doctor (Mainland usually 醫生)
  - 化妝室 (TW, common for restroom) ≈ 화장실 化粧室 restroom (Mainland 洗手間/衛生間)
  - 藥局 (TW) = 약국 藥局 pharmacy (Mainland 藥店/藥房)
  - Hokkien 寫真 siá-tsin "photo" = 사진 寫眞 photo
  - Counter-examples: Mainland 地鐵 is closer to 지하철 地下鐵 than Taiwan's 捷運; 汽車, 新聞 and others are false friends for everyone.

### Gaps
- No controlled or survey evidence on Taiwanese vs Mainland learners' Korean vocabulary speed.
- Could not confirm current (2026) Hanja use on Seoul Metro signage.

## Q3. Regular Mandarin to Sino-Korean sound correspondences: can a learner guess readings?

### Takeaway
Sino-Korean readings come from Middle Chinese, so they line up **systematically** with Mandarin, especially in syllable endings and many initials. Finals are the most reliable guide (-ng → ㅇ; entering-tone syllables → ㄱ/ㄹ/ㅂ). Mandarin's mergers (-m/-n, loss of -p/-t/-k, j/q/x from two sources) leave gaps that Mandarin alone can't resolve. Rules are best used to **recognize and remember**, not to produce words cold. Vowels are irregular, and Korean sound changes in speech (전화 → [저놔], 학생 → [학쌩]) can hide the link by ear.

### Cited Findings
- Korean Hanja readings derive from Chinese readings. The zh.wikipedia article gives a Middle Chinese to Korean rhyme correspondence table, including entering-tone -t → Korean -l (e.g., -al, -ol, -ul) — [維基百科：韓國漢字音](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hk/%E6%9C%9D%E9%AE%AE%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97%E9%9F%B3) (snippet).
- A study on the Korean and Chinese pronunciation of Chinese characters examines how far Korean and Chinese syllables correspond. It proposes that learners can infer unfamiliar Sino-Korean words from the Chinese syllables they already know — [PACLIC 2018, ACL Anthology Y18-1050](https://aclanthology.org/Y18-1050.pdf).
- A KCI study of Chinese learners' pronunciation errors on Sino-Korean words found **fewer errors where both sound and meaning are similar**, and concluded that "Hanja study is more effective for Korean learning when pronunciation is similar" — [중국인 학습자의 한국어와 중국어 유사발음 비교 분석 및 발음지도 연구 (KCI)](https://journal.kci.go.kr/kolali/archive/articlePdf?artiId=ART001425729).
- **두음법칙 (initial sound rule), 한글 맞춤법 제10항–12항:** Sino-Korean 녀·뇨·뉴·니 at the start of a word become 여·요·유·이 (여자 女子, 연세 年歲, 익명 匿名), but keep their original sound inside a word (남녀 男女, 당뇨 糖尿, 은닉 隱匿). The rule applies only to Sino-Korean words, not native words or loanwords — [대한민국 한글 맞춤법 (Wikisource)](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/%EB%8C%80%ED%95%9C%EB%AF%BC%EA%B5%AD_%ED%95%9C%EA%B8%80_%EB%A7%9E%EC%B6%A4%EB%B2%95(%EC%A0%9C2014-39%ED%98%B8)); [국립국어원 Q&A](https://www.korean.go.kr/nkview/nknews/199905/10_8.htm). (Articles 11–12 cover initial ㄹ → ㅇ before i/y and ㄹ → ㄴ elsewhere, e.g., 이유 理由, 노인 老人; that detail comes from my knowledge of the 맞춤법, not the snippet.)

### Inferences
- **Numbers are the single highest-return Sino-Korean set:** prices (만 원 萬, 천 千, 백 百), phone numbers, dates, subway/bus lines, minutes. All ten digits follow the rules above.
- Guessing a reading from Mandarin plus these rules usually gets the consonants right but often misses the vowel (生 생 vs 星 성, 京 경). Recognition works far better than production: hearing "운동" and thinking 運動 is easy; producing 星 → 성 from Mandarin is not. I found no source giving a guess-accuracy percentage.
- Korean sound changes at syllable joints obscure Sino-Korean words by ear: 학년 → [항년], 십육 → [심뉵], 입구 → [입꾸], 전화 → [저놔], 연락 → [열락]. Sentence-audio shadowing teaches these, and seeing the Hanja helps explain them.

### Gaps
- No source quantifying how often Mandarin-based guesses of Sino-Korean readings are correct (e.g., percentage of the 1,800 basic Hanja predictable from Mandarin). The ACL paper may report correspondence rates; I could not open it.

## Q4. Does Taiwanese Hokkien (Taigi) help more than Mandarin?

### Takeaway
Yes, for pronunciation. Hokkien keeps the Middle Chinese -m/-p/-t/-k endings and many older initials (b- for Mandarin w-, k- for Mandarin j/q), so Hokkien literary readings often sound strikingly like Korean: 三 sam / 삼, 十 si̍p / 십, 感謝 kám-siā / 감사, 時間 sî-kan / 시간. Taiwanese media and forums highlight this. Evidence is anecdotal or journalistic, not experimental. The learner benefits only to the extent they actually know Hokkien literary (文讀) readings.

### Cited Findings
- TVBS: 感謝, 時間, 派出所 sound nearly the same in Taiwanese and Korean, and Korean tourists in Taiwan correctly understood such Taiwanese words in a reporter's street test. The report attributes this to about 70% of Korean vocabulary being Hanja-based — [TVBS新聞網](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/970147) (journalistic test, not research).
- Taiwanese, Japanese and Korean all preserve more Middle Chinese features such as entering tones. Mandarin has none, while Taiwanese, Hakka and Cantonese keep them — [Dcard 語言版：台日韓語讀音共同點比較](https://www.dcard.tw/f/language/p/235749037); [台語入聲字 blog](https://yifertw.blogspot.com/2011/08/1_28.html).
- Other popular pieces list near-identical pairs such as 시간 (時間) and 준비 (準備) — [yam 蕃薯藤：學韓語前先學好台語？](https://dq.yam.com/post/15160); [k.urcook.com](https://k.urcook.com/28/); [YouTube: 韓語其實是台語？](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=615wbdFgRT4).
- zh.wikipedia states Cantonese speakers usually pick up Korean Hanja readings faster, and a Hong Kong blog describes regular Cantonese–Korean conversions such as -t → -l. Cantonese, like Hokkien, keeps -p/-t/-k/-m — [維基百科：韓國漢字音](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hk/%E6%9C%9D%E9%AE%AE%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97%E9%9F%B3); [論韓文漢字音與廣東話之相似性](https://projectcornwall.drunkafternoon.com/archives/1901).
- The same sources warn that some similar-sounding words differ in meaning (e.g., 新聞), and that grammar and writing differ greatly, so the advantage is in pronunciation and vocabulary, not overall learning — [TVBS](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/970147) (as summarized in search results).

- The simplest Hokkien rule for this learner: **Hokkien -t → Korean ㄹ; -p → ㅂ; -k → ㄱ; -m → ㅁ; -n → ㄴ; -ng → ㅇ.** Of these, only the -t → ㄹ step changes the sound.
- Caveats *[linguistic knowledge]*: (1) Colloquial (白讀) Hokkien readings often diverge (十 tsa̍p, 八 peh, 鐵 thih); literary (文讀) readings match Korean better, and many Taiwanese under 30 mainly know colloquial forms. (2) Exceptions exist: 法 is huat in Hokkien (-t) but 법 in Korean (-p). (3) Hokkien h- covers Mandarin f- (法 huat, 方 hong) while Korean uses ㅂ, so Mandarin/Hokkien initials don't help there.
- Since the learner left Taiwan at 14 and Hokkien knowledge varies, budget this as a **memory hook** (hear "삼", think "sam") rather than a system to rely on.

### Gaps
- No experimental or survey study showing Hokkien-speaking Taiwanese learn Korean faster than Mandarin-only Taiwanese.

## Q5. False friends and meaning shifts

### Takeaway
Sino-Korean words with the same Hanja often differ in meaning, scope, part of speech or register. Research on the mirror direction (Koreans learning Chinese) finds that same-form, same-meaning words transfer best, while same-form words with different meaning breadth cause the most errors. A learner should keep a short "false friend" list in their deck, especially for words that come up in plans and meetups (약속, 공부, 방학).

### Cited Findings
- Survey research (Korean learners of Chinese, Taiwan thesis): same-form same-meaning words had the highest accuracy and transfer positively. Same-form words with broader Korean meaning were most prone to L1 interference, and those with broader Chinese meaning caused the most negative transfer. Even same-form words often differ in part of speech and syntactic role — [airiti thesis U0021-1610201315293656](https://www.airitilibrary.com/Publication/Index/U0021-1610201315293656); [NCCU repository](https://irtest.lib.nccu.edu.tw/retrieve/82099/102201.pdf) (as summarized in search results; reverse direction of our case).
- Hakka and Korean also share same-form, different-meaning words — same search result set ([airiti U0017-1511201111455115](https://www.airitilibrary.com/Article/Detail/U0017-1511201111455115)).
- Korean speakers often assume Sino-Korean words equal their Chinese counterparts, leading to semantic and usage errors; changed-meaning characters are prone to negative transfer — [corpus-based study of Korean vocabulary acquisition](https://webofproceedings.org/proceedings_series/article/artId/23687.html) (summary only).
- 新聞 differs in meaning between the two languages — [TVBS](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/970147) (summary).
- Similar-looking Hanja can mislead even in names (a scene in 《非常律師禹英禑》 is cited as an example) — [The News Lens / CommonWealth Crossing articles via search](https://crossing.cw.com.tw/article/16510) (summary; weak relevance).

## Q6. Evidence that Chinese-L1 (and Japanese-L1) learners learn Korean vocabulary faster or reach TOPIK levels sooner

### Takeaway
There is **broad expert consensus and anecdote** that Hanja-culture learners (Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese) have a vocabulary advantage. Korean pedagogy treats 한자권 and 비한자권 learners as separate groups. But I found **no controlled study or TOPIK pass-rate data by nationality** proving that Chinese-L1 learners reach levels faster than English-L1 learners. Japanese learners are generally seen as having the biggest overall head start, because grammar (SOV word order, particles, honorifics) also transfers, while Chinese grammar does not.

### Cited Findings
- Korean-pedagogy literature routinely separates Hanja-sphere from non-Hanja-sphere learners, e.g., [비한자권 한국어 학습자 대상 한자어 교육 실행 연구 (Ewha DSpace)](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/260328); [발견학습을 활용한 한국어 한자어 교육방안 연구: 초급 중국인 학습자 대상 (ScienceON)](https://scienceon.kisti.re.kr/srch/selectPORSrchArticle.do?cn=DIKO0015926144&dbt=DIKO); [고급 단계 중국인 한국어 학습자의 한자어 어휘 교수-학습 전략 연구](https://scienceon.kisti.re.kr/srch/selectPORSrchArticle.do?cn=DIKO0011404749); [베트남 중급 학습자를 위한 한자어 교육 방법 연구 (한성대 2026)](https://dspace.hansung.ac.kr/bitstream/2024.oak/10570/2/TH_I1A5A5_%EB%B2%A0%ED%8A%B8%EB%82%A8%20%EC%A4%91%EA%B8%89%20%ED%95%99%EC%8A%B5%EC%9E%90%EB%A5%BC%20%EC%9C%84%ED%95%9C%20%ED%95%9C%EC%9E%90%EC%96%B4%20%EA%B5%90%EC%9C%A1%20%EB%B0%A9%EB%B2%95%20%EC%97%B0%EA%B5%AC%20.pdf).
- A KCI paper on a Hanja-word difficulty hierarchy for Japanese learners states it is "the general view" that Japanese learners find Korean vocabulary easy because of shared Hanja words. It also notes that phonological differences between Korean and Japanese Hanja readings make it hard to bridge L1 and target words — [한국어교육을 위한 한자어 난이도 위계 설정 - 일본인 학습자를 중심으로 (KCI)](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART002359093).
- Chinese learners tend to understand Korean by linking it to Chinese, and Hanja study helps more when pronunciation is similar — [KCI ksclc article on learners' Hanja-word strategies](https://journal.kci.go.kr/ksclc/archive/articlePdf?artiId=ART002402628); [KCI kolali pronunciation study](https://journal.kci.go.kr/kolali/archive/articlePdf?artiId=ART001425729).
- Shared Hanja vocabulary speeds acquisition but increases interference errors. Stated for Korean learners of Chinese/Japanese/Vietnamese — [기초학문자료센터: 학습자의 한자어 습득 능력이 한자문화권 언어 학습에 미치는 영향](https://www.krm.or.kr/krmts/search/detailView.html?dbGubun=SD&category=Report&m201_id=10059467&local_id=10081908).
- TOPIK applicants by country (year not stated, wiki-type source): **China 60,834 (1st), Vietnam 35,427 (2nd), Japan 32,993 (3rd)** — [나무위키: TOPIK](https://namu.wiki/w/TOPIK). Official yearly applicant/taker/passer files exist at [data.go.kr (국립국제교육원, as of 2024-12-31)](https://www.data.go.kr/data/3059526/fileData.do) and [교육부](https://moe.go.kr/boardCnts/view.do?boardID=350&boardSeq=75623&lev=0&searchType=null&statusYN=C&page=1&s=moe&m=0309&opType=N). I could not open them, and search snippets gave no pass rates by nationality.
- Blog-level claims that shared morphemes "accelerate Korean vocabulary acquisition substantially" for Chinese/Japanese-background learners — [torontocall.com](https://torontocall.com/sino-korean-vocabulary-why-it-matters-in-language-learning/) (informal).

### Inferences
- *[general knowledge, unsourced]*: The U.S. Foreign Service Institute rates Korean among its hardest languages for English speakers (Category IV, about 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours). No equivalent official estimate exists for Chinese speakers.
- Japanese vs Chinese advantage *[inference]*: Japanese speakers gain on **both** vocabulary (Sino-Japanese words, many coined in Meiji Japan and borrowed into Korean, e.g., 寫眞, 自動車, 化粧室) **and** grammar. Chinese speakers gain mainly on **nouns**, plus some phonology (Korean keeps -p/-t/-k/-m much like Hokkien/Cantonese; Japanese lost -m/-p). Japanese on'yomi is phonetically further from Korean than Hokkien is (十 jū vs 십 vs si̍p), which matches the KCI paper's note on phonological mismatch.
- For a one-month trip, the Chinese-L1 edge mostly means **faster retention of nouns, numbers, place and transit words**, and easier guessing of signs and announcements once Hangul is learned. It does not speed up grammar, verb conjugation or listening to fast casual speech, where most of the difficulty lies (Q1: about 85% of spoken tokens are not Sino-Korean).

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing vocabulary learning speed of Chinese-L1 vs English-L1 Korean learners.
- No TOPIK pass rate or level distribution by nationality found (official files exist but couldn't be opened).
- No direct Japanese vs Chinese learner speed comparison found.

## Q7. Practical ways to exploit Sino-Korean vocabulary in a sentence-based method

### Takeaway
Best returns: (1) learn Hangul first (Hanja are useless without it); (2) annotate the *Sino-Korean nouns* in each sentence card with Hanja; (3) grow small "root families" from high-frequency syllables (學 학, 電 전, 動 동, 場 장, 口 구, 國 국, 人 인); (4) learn the Sino-Korean number system early; (5) keep a false-friend flag. Free tools: Naver Hanja dictionary (hanja.dict.naver.com) for roots and related words. Paid: TTMIK's Hanja book, *Root Korean*.

### Cited Findings
- Naver's Hanja dictionary (hanja.dict.naver.com, plus app) shows a word's Hanja and lists other Korean words built from the same character. Learners recommend pasting new vocabulary into it to see related words — [GoBillyKorean: 10 ways to use Naver dictionary](https://gobillykorean.com/10-ways-to-use-naver-dictionary-to-support-your-korean-study/); [WaniKani forum: "Wanikani korean"](https://community.wanikani.com/t/wanikani-korean/39589).
- *Root Korean: A Dictionary of Korean Root Words (Hanja)* (book + Android app) is organized by single-syllable root, e.g., look up 입 and 구 to decode 입구 — [Everand listing](https://www.everand.com/book/378719703/Root-Korean-A-Dictionary-of-Korean-Root-Words-Hanja).
- Talk To Me In Korean (TTMIK) sells a beginner Hanja book (about US$27.95) — [Speechling blog: Korean Hanja – How I learned](https://speechling.com/blog/korean-hanja-how-i-learned-hanja-and-how-you-can-too).
- Meridian Linguistics offers a short free "Introduction to Hanja" course, including how to use Naver's Hanja dictionary — [Thinkific course page](https://meridianlinguistics.thinkific.com/courses/introduction-to-hanja).
- Other tools: Okpyeon Hanja popup dictionary (Chrome extension) — [Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/enjmfjcemaiabocebnfokcmglfmnfjhl); hangulhanja.com Korean–English dictionary with Hanja — [hangulhanja.com](https://hangulhanja.com/en).
- No ready-made Hanja-annotated Anki deck for Chinese speakers turned up in searches. Making your own with Naver as the lookup source is the usual path — [same search set](https://expatkorea33.blogspot.com/2025/12/resources-for-learning-hanja-as-korean.html).
- A Taiwanese Korean-major student on Dcard warns against learning pronunciation via Chinese-character "空耳" (Chinese-character approximations of sounds), because it trains wrong pronunciation — [Dcard 語言版](https://www.dcard.tw/f/language/p/242648574) (summary).
- Experimental work on Chinese learners supports discovery-style Hanja-word teaching at the beginner level — [발견학습을 활용한 한국어 한자어 교육방안 연구](https://scienceon.kisti.re.kr/srch/selectPORSrchArticle.do?cn=DIKO0015926144&dbt=DIKO) (title/abstract level only).

### Inferences: suggested card and deck design *[my synthesis]*
- **Card format (sentence-first, Hanja as a hint, not the main prompt):**
  - Front: audio + Hangul sentence, e.g., `지하철 2호선 타요.`
  - Back: English, plus a Hanja gloss line on the Sino-Korean words only: `지하철 地下鐵 · 2호선 二號線`, plus a false-friend flag if needed (⚠ 약속 ≠ 約束 restrain). Native words get no gloss.
- **Root families worth building in week 1** (each is one card set or one note per root):
  - 學 학: 학교 學校 school, 학생 學生 student, 대학교 大學校 university, 방학 放學 vacation ⚠, 유학 留學 study abroad
  - 電 전: 전화 電話 phone, 충전 充電 / 충전기 充電器 charger, 전기 電氣 electricity, 전철 電鐵 commuter train
  - 動 동: 운동 運動 exercise, 운동화 運動靴 sneakers, 동영상 動映像 video clip, 자동차 自動車 car
  - 場 장: 운동장 運動場 sports ground, 주차장 駐車場 parking lot, 스케이트장 (skate + 場) skatepark, 시장 市場 market
  - 口 구: 입구 入口 entrance, 출구 出口 exit, 비상구 非常口 emergency exit
  - 國/人 국/인: 한국 韓國, 대만 臺灣, 외국인 外國人 foreigner, 한국인 韓國人
  - 號 호: 번호 番號 number, 전화번호 電話番號 phone number, 호선 號線 subway line
- **Numbers:** drill Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼 … 십, 백, 천, 만) with prices (만 원), phone numbers and 호선 in week 1. Native numbers (하나, 둘, 셋) are a separate set used for counting things, hours and age.
- **Hokkien hook:** if the learner knows Taigi, note Hokkien readings on cards where they help (삼 sam, 십 si̍p, 감사 kám-siā, 학 ha̍k).
- **Order of operations for someone arriving tomorrow:** (1) Hangul, a few hours; (2) about 30 survival sentences with audio (greetings, 얼마예요?, 화장실 어디예요?, skater intros: "저 대만에서 왔어요", "같이 타도 돼요?", "그 기술 뭐예요?"); (3) add Hanja glosses to the Sino-Korean nouns in those sentences; (4) grow root families from words met in the wild, looked up in Naver's Hanja dictionary.
- **Limits to state honestly:** Hanja won't help with grammar endings, native verbs/adjectives, English-loan trick names, slang, or fast casual listening. Relying on Hanja-based words can also sound formal; casual speech prefers native words.

### Gaps
- No evidence on whether Hanja-annotated sentence cards actually improve retention for Chinese speakers compared with plain cards; no study found.
- Could not verify current (late 2026) prices or availability of TTMIK's Hanja book or the *Root Korean* app.

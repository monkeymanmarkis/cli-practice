## 1. FSI difficulty rating and what 1 month can realistically achieve

### Takeaway
Korean sits in the FSI's hardest tier for English speakers, at about 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours to reach professional working proficiency (ILR 3). One month of self-study by a beginner who arrives tomorrow is about 1-5% of that. A realistic goal is survival-level speech plus a set of memorized, personally relevant chunks, not conversation.

### Cited Findings
- Korean is commonly cited as needing about 2,200 class hours (88 weeks) for English speakers. One source frames this as 88 weeks at 25 hours a week. — [FluentU](https://www.fluentu.com/blog/korean/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-korean/); [Atlas & Boots](https://www.atlasandboots.com/?p=83528); [Noun Town](https://noun.town/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-korean/)
- Sources disagree on the category label. A 2017 Korea Times article calls Korean "Category IV" alongside Arabic, Chinese and Japanese, while several blogs call it "Category V." — [Korea Times 2017](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/nation/2017/12/181_240261.html); contradicted by blogs such as [Ling App](https://ling-app.com/blog/hardest-language-to-learn-for-english-speakers/)
- The 2,200-hour figure is for classroom hours to reach professional working proficiency (about ILR Level 3), not basic conversation. — [Atlas & Boots summary of FSI](https://www.atlasandboots.com/?p=83528)
- The official FSI page (state.gov/foreign-language-training) could not be fetched in this session, so the exact current wording was not verified first-hand.

### Inferences
- The "Category IV vs V" conflict likely comes from FSI's older 5-category scheme versus the current 4-category list, in which Category IV is labeled "super-hard languages." The writer should use "Category IV (the hardest tier, about 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours)" and note that some sites say "Category V." [Partly UNSOURCED - background knowledge]
- At about 1-3 hours a day for 30 days (30-90 hours), the learner can realistically: read Hangul (slowly), produce about 50-150 memorized chunks in 해요체, handle ordering, transit, greetings, numbers and prices, and have a few scripted skatepark exchanges (praise, asking to join, asking a trick name, introducing themselves). Unscripted listening will stay very weak.
- For the sentence-memorization approach, Korean's distance from English is the main argument for learning chunks. The learner cannot build sentences from rules in a month, so pre-built, personally relevant sentences give the best return.

### Gaps
- No first-hand fetch of the FSI page. The current category wording is inferred.
- No study found that measures what learners reach in 1 month of immersion in Korea.

## 2. Hangul: how long it takes, why first, why romanization is a trap

### Takeaway
The basics of Hangul can be learned in hours to a few days. It should come first because romanization is inconsistent, hides the plain/aspirated/tense contrasts and sound changes, and will make the learner's memorized sentences sound wrong.

### Cited Findings
- "The basics of the Korean script can be learned in a matter of hours," but finer points of pronunciation and spacing can take years. — [Korean Wiki Project, Hangeul step 1](https://koreanwikiproject.com/wiki/Hangeul_step_1)
- Not everyone uses the same romanization system, a romanized spelling may not show the correct sound, and learners who don't know Hangul are "likely to mispronounce" words. — [Rocket Languages forum](https://www.rocketlanguages.com/forum/general/romanization-and-korean-words)
- Learners on italki say that learning only romanization "is no help." — [italki discussion 54048](https://www.italki.com/es/post/discussion-54048)
- Because the writing system matches pronunciation so closely, guides argue Hangul must be learned first. — [Stepping Stone Korean (Substack)](https://steppingstonekorean.substack.com/p/why-you-should-learn-how-to-read); [Kaiwa: Korean learning mistakes](https://trykaiwa.com/blog/korean-learning-mistakes)
- Spelling does not always show pronunciation. For example, 학교 (school) is pronounced [학꾜] because of tensification. — [elon.io: tensification](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/hangul/tensification-preview)

### Inferences
- Practical reasons Hangul matters for this learner in Seoul. Signs, menus, subway station names (also shown in English), and skate Instagram captions are all in Hangul. Korean Konglish skate terms written in Hangul (for example 킥플립, kickflip) are instantly readable once Hangul is known, which is a quick win.
- Romanization collapses ㄱ/ㅋ/ㄲ (g/k/kk) and ㅓ/ㅗ (eo/o) into letters an English reader will say with English sounds. A learner who memorizes "eo" will usually say "ee-oh" or "oh" and lose the distinction.
- Recommendation for day 1-3: learn Hangul with a free resource (for example Talk To Me In Korean's Hangul lessons or How to Study Korean Unit 0), then write all memorized sentences in Hangul only. [Resource names are UNSOURCED - background knowledge; the sites were not fetched]

### Gaps
- "Learn Hangul in 1-2 hours" claims are mostly marketing. No rigorous data found on how long it takes to become a fluent reader.

## 3. Speech levels, banmal, 말 놓을까요, age hierarchy

### Takeaway
A foreign beginner should default to 해요체 (polite -요 endings) with every stranger, including young skaters. Switching to 반말 (casual speech) happens by explicit agreement, usually after ages are known, and is often proposed by the older person, with phrases like 말 놓을까요? ("Shall we speak casually?") or 말 편하게 해요 ("Speak comfortably/casually"). 합쇼체 (-습니다) is for formal and announcement contexts, and the learner mostly needs to recognize it, not produce it.

### Cited Findings
- 말 놓을까요 is a proposal to drop polite speech and switch to banmal ("shall we speak casually?"). The switch is negotiated out loud. — [elon.io: 말 놓기, negotiating the switch to 반말](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/pragmatics/malnoki-banmal-negotiation)
- Peers of the same age still start in 존댓말 (polite speech) as strangers. Same age "permits an easy switch to 반말, but only after you actually make it." Agreement "licenses 반말; it doesn't obligate it." — [elon.io: when 반말 is allowed](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/honorifics/when-banmal-licensed)
- Banmal generally fits close friends, family, and people of the same age group where familiarity has been established. Age is asked early so both sides know where they stand. — [NLS Norwegian: jondaetmal vs banmal](https://nlsnorwegian.no/jondaetmal-vs-banmal-navigating-korean-speech-levels/)
- Asking someone's age is not rude in South Korea, because age decides speech level and forms of address. — [DT Next: why asking "how old are you" isn't rude in South Korea](https://www.dtnext.in/amp/story/edit/culture-schlock-why-asking-how-old-are-you-isn-t-rude-in-south-korea)
- The complexity of speech levels, honorifics and the emphasis on age "often overwhelm learners." Some Korean educators argue the jondaemal/banmal structure hinders equal relationships. — [Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/3211101)
- Making friends realistically in Korean involves the age question and a negotiated switch. — [Kaiwa: making friends in Korean](https://trykaiwa.com/blog/korean-making-friends-realistic); [Kaiwa: phrases foreigners get wrong (2026)](https://trykaiwa.com/blog/korean-phrases-foreigners-get-wrong-2026)

### Key Hangul for the learner (meanings are standard. Phrases are UNSOURCED beyond the 말 놓을까요 pattern above)
- 몇 살이에요? = How old are you? (Koreans may instead ask 몇 년생이에요? = What year were you born?)
- 저는 [year]년생이에요 = I was born in [year].
- 형 (hyung) = older brother, used by males for older males. 누나 (nuna) = older sister, used by males for older females. 오빠 (oppa) = older brother, used by females for older males. 언니 (eonni) = older sister, used by females for older females. These are used for close friends, not only relatives.
- 말 놓을까요? / 말 편하게 할까요? = Shall we speak casually?
- 편하게 말해요 / 말 놓으세요 = Feel free to talk casually (to me).
- Speech levels: 감사합니다 (합쇼체, formal) / 고마워요 (해요체, polite) / 고마워 (반말, casual) = thank you.

### Inferences
- Risk of banmal with strangers. Unprompted banmal from an adult stranger can read as rude, condescending, or picking a fight, especially toward someone older. Foreigners get some leeway, but the learner shouldn't rely on it. Memorizing sentences only in banmal (common in K-drama-based learning) is the biggest register trap.
- Skate-scene nuance [UNSOURCED - background knowledge, plausible but unverified]: skateparks are informal, youth-heavy spaces, and teens and 20-somethings who session together often move to banmal quickly once ages are known. Younger skaters may call an older foreign skater 형 or 누나. The safest script is to start in 해요체 and let the Korean skater offer the switch.
- For the sentence method, the learner should keep each core sentence in 해요체, then add a banmal version of a few high-frequency lines (praise, "one more time," "let's go") for when a friendship forms.
- Short exclamations (나이스, 대박, 와) have no speech level, so they are safe with anyone.

### Gaps
- No source specific to Korean skaters' speech-level norms was found.
- The sources on how fast peers switch are language-school blogs with mixed advice on timing.

## 4. Grammar features that limit pure sentence memorization

### Takeaway
Korean is SOV, marks roles with particles, packs tense, politeness and mood into verb endings, and drops subjects freely. Memorized sentences cannot easily be recombined without a small amount of explicit grammar. The minimum useful kit: particle function (은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 에/에서), verb-final order, and the -아요/-어요 polite present plus a few endings (-고 싶어요, -ㄹ 수 있어요, -해 주세요, -ㄹ까요?).

### Cited Findings
- Korean word order is Subject-Object-Verb, and particles carry the grammatical roles that word order does in English. 나는 사과를 먹어요 ("I eat an apple") means the same even if words are rearranged. — [Promova: complete guide to Korean particles](https://promova.com/blog/korean-particles); [LingoDeer: 은/는 vs 이/가](https://blog.lingodeer.com/?p=1792)
- Once a subject has been mentioned it can be dropped in later sentences. Learners report confusion when Koreans "would drop the subject entirely." — [UBC Korean: sentence subjects and topics](https://korean.sites.olt.ubc.ca/?p=7386); [KoreanClass101 forum](https://koreanclass101.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=11073)
- Textbooks teach particles as obligatory, but casual speech drops them often when context is clear. — [KoreanClass101: dropping particles](https://www.koreanclass101.com/lesson/absolute-beginner-questions-answered-by-jae-3-dropping-particles)
- 이/가 is the default when introducing a subject. Once a topic is established, speakers switch to 은/는 or drop the subject. Rule: 은/이 follow a consonant, 는/가 follow a vowel. — [LingoDeer](https://blog.lingodeer.com/?p=1792); [Promova](https://promova.com/blog/korean-particles)
- Caution: one site (Sublearn) reverses the 이/가 rule and is wrong. — [Sublearn subject-marker page](https://sublearn.com/learn/ko/grammar/ko-subject-particle)

### Inferences
- Agglutination works both ways for this method. It's hard because one verb shows up in many forms (가요 / 갔어요 / 갈래요? / 가고 싶어요 / 가자, all built on 가다 "go"). It also helps, because a few templates repeat endlessly: [noun] 주세요 (please give me ___), [verb]-고 싶어요 (I want to ___), [verb]-아/어도 돼요? (Is it OK if I ___?), [verb]-ㄹ 수 있어요? (Can you/I ___?). Memorizing sentences built on these frames turns them into slots. [UNSOURCED - background knowledge on the templates]
- Subject dropping helps production (the learner can say 배우고 싶어요, "(I) want to learn," without 저는), but it hurts listening.
- Verdict for this learner: yes, Korean needs more explicit grammar than, say, Spanish to make memorized sentences reusable. About 2-3 hours total on particles plus the 4-5 ending frames above is enough to start swapping words.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found comparing pure chunk memorization with explicit grammar for Korean beginners.

## 5. Pronunciation hurdles and implications for shadowing and TTS

### Takeaway
The biggest hurdle is the three-way stop contrast: plain (ㄱㄷㅂㅈㅅ), aspirated (ㅋㅌㅍㅊ) and tense (ㄲㄸㅃㅉㅆ). It is based on breath and tension, not voicing. Next come the vowels ㅓ/ㅗ (and ㅡ), and sound changes across batchim (final consonants) that make spoken Korean differ from its spelling. Shadowing native or high-quality TTS audio of each memorized sentence, rather than reading it, is especially important for Korean.

### Cited Findings
- Korean has no phonemic voicing. It sorts stops by breath and tension into plain / aspirated / tense. — [elon.io: consonants, a three-way contrast](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/hangul/consonants-overview); [Wikipedia: Korean phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_phonology)
- Per a Seoul National University record, the hardest Korean consonants for English speakers to perceive and produce are the plain versus aspirated stops. — [SNU S-Space](https://s-space.snu.ac.kr/handle/10371/87181?mode=full)
- English speakers usually learn the aspirated set fast (close to English k/t/p) but blur plain and tense together. Many map ㄲ onto English /k/ with a puff of air, so it comes out like ㅋ. — [elon.io: tense consonants](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/hangul/consonants-tense); [Kaiwa: pronunciation mistakes English speakers make (2026)](https://trykaiwa.com/blog/korean-pronunciation-mistakes-english-speakers-2026)
- ㅓ, ㅗ and ㅡ are listed among the hardest vowels for English speakers. — [Kaiwa 2026](https://trykaiwa.com/blog/korean-pronunciation-mistakes-english-speakers-2026)
- Tensification: a plain stop right after a stop batchim becomes tense (학교 sounds like [학꾜]). — [elon.io: tensification](https://elon.io/grammar/korean/hangul/tensification-preview)
- Nasalization: final ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ before ㄴ/ㅁ become ㅇ/ㄴ/ㅁ (for example 감사합니다 is said [감사함니다]). — [Wikibooks: Korean essential pronunciation rules](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Korean/Essential_Pronunciation_Rules); the example is standard (Wikipedia phonology)
- Several pronunciation sources (talkpal.ai, some blogs) mislabel the consonant categories. Wikipedia and elon.io are more reliable. — [Wikipedia: Korean phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_phonology)

### Inferences
- Liaison (연음): a final consonant carries over to a following vowel. 한국어 is said [한구거] and 옷이 is said [오시]. Combined with nasalization and tensification, the learner's ear will not match the spelling, so each memorized sentence needs audio. [Example forms UNSOURCED - standard textbook rules]
- ㅓ vs ㅗ: ㅓ is an open, unrounded "uh/aw" (lips relaxed), and ㅗ is a rounded "o" (lips pushed forward). Mixing them changes words (for example 거 vs 고). [UNSOURCED - background knowledge]
- Good news for the learner: Korean has no lexical tone (unlike Mandarin), and its syllable shapes are fairly simple, so intelligibility is reachable even if the stop contrast is imperfect. Context usually saves you.
- TTS implications: modern neural Korean TTS (for example Naver Papago, Google, or device voices) generally applies liaison, nasalization and tensification correctly, so it's fine for shadowing standard sentences. TTS will not capture fast casual slurring or skater slang intonation. Verify unusual loanwords (킥플립) by listening to Korean skate YouTube. [UNSOURCED - TTS quality not tested in this session]
- Shadowing tip specific to Korean: drill minimal triplets (가/카/까, 달/탈/딸, 불/풀/뿔) for 5 minutes a day early on. Tense sounds have no puff of air and a tight throat, and plain initial stops have a slight puff and a lower pitch on the following vowel.

### Gaps
- No good source on ㅓ/ㅗ articulation was retrieved in this session.
- TTS accuracy for Korean sound-change rules was not tested.

## 6. Korean skateboarding vocabulary and park phrases

### Takeaway
Korean skate vocabulary is overwhelmingly English loanwords written in Hangul. Trick and obstacle names (킥플립, 힐플립, 그라인드, 50-50, 매뉴얼, 드롭인, 레일, 뱅크, 볼) are recognizable once Hangul is read, and the learner mainly needs to say them with Korean syllable structure. Native Korean words carry the social glue: praise, requests, and joining a session. No Korean-language skate-slang glossary from a Korean skate outlet could be retrieved in this session, so slang items are flagged.

### Cited Findings
- Korean-language explanations of 올리 (ollie) describe it as the most basic trick, jumping with the board using only feet and weight shift, and the "father of skateboard tricks." 킥플립 (kickflip) is described as an ollie plus a front-foot flick that spins the board 360 degrees along its long axis. Most underlying sources were English. — search summary of [Wikipedia: Kickflip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kickflip) and [Langeek word list](https://langeek.co/en-KO/vocab/subcategory/5874/word-list)
- Korean summaries use 스팟 (spot, a street skating location) and 기물 (obstacles/features such as rails, stairs, ledges, benches), plus loanwords like 베일 (bail), 뱅크 (bank), 볼 (bowl), 드롭인 (drop in), 페이키 (fakie), 프론트사이드/백사이드 (frontside/backside). — search summary drawing on [SurferToday skate glossary](https://www.surfertoday.com/skateboarding/the-glossary-of-skateboarding-terms-and-slang) and [Wikipedia: Street skateboarding](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Street_skateboarding). Korean usage of 기물 was not confirmed by a Korean skate source.
- Korean lesson listings (Soomgo) advise beginners to learn 주행 (riding/cruising) before 트릭 (tricks), and use 스탠다드 보드 (standard board) vs 롱보드 (longboard). — [Soomgo skateboard lessons, Seoul Yongsan](https://soomgo.com/a.%EC%84%9C%EC%9A%B8-%EC%9A%A9%EC%82%B0%EA%B5%AC/%EC%8A%A4%EC%BC%80%EC%9D%B4%ED%8A%B8%EB%B3%B4%EB%93%9C-%EA%B0%95%EC%8A%B5)
- Konglish is English loanwords written and pronounced in Korean, and the meanings don't always match English. 파이팅 (fighting) is a cheer meaning "you can do it / good luck." — [The Smart Local: Konglish words](https://thesmartlocal.com/korea/konglish-words); [KoreanClass101: slang using English words](https://www.koreanclass101.com/lesson/mustknow-korean-slang-words-phrases-25-phrases-using-english-words)
- No source was found documenting 나이스 (nice) as skatepark usage, or a Korean skater slang list. — searches noted above returned nothing specific.

### Inferences
- For the sentence method, the skate domain is unusually friendly. Trick names are free vocabulary once Hangul is known, and many exchanges are short exclamations. The memorization effort should go to roughly 15-20 social frames (joining, asking, praising, filming, Instagram swap) in 해요체.
- Pronunciation tip: Korean loanwords add vowels to break consonant clusters (kickflip = 킥플립 "kik-peul-lip"). Saying the English version usually works at a skatepark, and the Hangul version shows effort and is better understood by non-English speakers.

## 7. Seoul skate scene: parks, spots, shops, crews, rules, where to meet skaters

### Takeaway
The best bets for meeting skaters are the Han River park X-game zones, especially Ttukseom (뚝섬한강공원 X-게임장, the largest, near Ttukseom Resort/Jayang station on Line 7). Others are Gwangnaru and Ichon Han River parks, the long-standing Dongdaemun-area "Cult" street/park spot, and plazas such as DDP, COEX and Yeouinaru, plus local shops. Much English-language info is 2013-2022 vintage, so the learner should check current Instagram before going.

### Cited Findings

- "Seoul Plaza" in the brief probably refers to the winter skating rink, which is ice skating, not a skateboard spot. The writer should not list it as a skate spot.
- Olympic Park (올림픽공원): no source in this session confirmed a skate facility there. It is a spacious plaza area where skating may happen, but treat it as unverified. Daehwa Park (대화공원) in the Rollernews video is likely Ilsan/Goyang, just outside Seoul. [UNCERTAIN]
- Etiquette [UNSOURCED - general skate etiquette plus Korean norms]: watch the flow and take turns (no "snaking," that is, cutting in), don't sit on obstacles, nod or say 안녕하세요 when arriving, and leave plazas when security (경비 / 보안요원) asks. Korean apartment complexes, malls and private plazas often post 보드 금지 / 스케이트보드 금지 (no skateboards) or 킥보드·인라인·자전거 금지 signs, and the learner should recognize these words. Cult's history of resident complaints suggests noise complaints (민원) are the main friction point.
- Where to find skaters to talk to: (1) Ttukseom X-game zone on weekend afternoons and evenings, (2) Cult or Dongdaemun and DDP in the evening, (3) local skate shops (ask staff about sessions.

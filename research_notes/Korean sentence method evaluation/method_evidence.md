# Evidence Review: The "Personal Sentence List" Language Method (adult English-speaking total beginner)

Method being evaluated: (1) narrate your daily life with speech-to-text, then AI-translate it into a personal sentence list; (2) AI text-to-speech audio played on repeat in dead time, plus shadowing; (3) active recall from English to the target language, said aloud, then checked. Side claims: "no grammar at all", Duolingo is a waste of time, passive immersion barely works, pre-study a transcript before native content, "90% never reach basic conversation", "conversational in 6 weeks", "reviewing before sleep dramatically improves retention".

Research note: about 19 search calls. Most findings come from abstracts, publisher pages and secondary summaries, not full texts. Several hosts (e.g. ir.lib.uwo.ca) could not be reached from this session.

## Q1. Chunks, formulaic sequences and whole sentences vs single words: does memorizing sentences generalize into grammar?

### Takeaway
This is partly supported. Learning formulaic sequences (chunks) clearly helps, and learner-tracking research shows memorized chunks are gradually "unpacked" into productive grammar. That process is slow, uneven and incomplete. It works alongside rule-building rather than replacing it, so a sentence list is a good scaffold but does not on its own yield generative grammar quickly.

### Cited Findings
- Boers & Lindstromberg (2012) review experimental and intervention studies on L2 formulaic sequences published since 2004 in *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics*. It is a widely cited review, not a meta-analysis. — [Cambridge Core, ARAL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/experimental-and-intervention-studies-on-formulaic-sequences-in-a-second-language/A2ACDF54604CFAC4443240748360C403)
- A 2021 *IRAL* meta-analysis of instruction for L2 collocation learning pooled 64 research projects in 17 primary studies (N = 3,859). The search snippet confirms instruction was examined but I could not retrieve the pooled effect size. — [De Gruyter, IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2021-0218/html)
- A small-scale meta-analysis by Boers and colleagues found that assonance (sound repetition) has an initially medium-sized effect on learning formulaic sequences. This shows that the form of a chunk affects how well it is remembered. — [Benjamins, ITL](https://benjamins.com/catalog/itl.165.1.02lin)
- Myles, Hooper & Mitchell (1998, *Language Learning*, "Rote or rule?") followed 16 school-age classroom beginners of French for about two years. Most gradually "unpacked" early chunks such as *comment t'appelles-tu?* and reused parts of them productively. The authors concluded that "rote-learning of formulas and the construction of rules are not independent processes, but interact and feed into one another." — [Essex/Myles summary PDF](https://www.essex.ac.uk/-/media/documents/departments/langling/myles-elct-15.pdf); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/4203/)
- In the same research line, even after three years of teaching only one learner had consistently mastered the final stage of the question-formation sequence. Segmentation of chunks into grammar is described as taking "a long time, even with support from instruction." — [Myles ELCT PDF](https://www.essex.ac.uk/-/media/documents/departments/langling/myles-elct-15.pdf); [Essex "learning for life" PDF](https://www1.essex.ac.uk/langling/documents/elct/2012/learning_for_life.pdf)
- Myles (2012) argues that chunks are used as models to build a productive L2 grammar. Learners also fall back on rote chunks when their grammar cannot meet communicative needs. — [Essex "learning for life" PDF](https://www1.essex.ac.uk/langling/documents/elct/2012/learning_for_life.pdf)
- Psycholinguistically defined formulaic sequences make up about 27% of advanced English learners' French, according to Cordier's doctoral study. — [Newcastle thesis PDF](https://dspace.ncl.ac.uk/jspui/bitstream/10443/2265/1/Cordier%2013.pdf)
- Corpus frequency predicts learners' knowledge of collocations (Durrant 2014 meta-analysis, *International Journal of Corpus Linguistics*). This is cited in a reference list only. — [search snippet via Benjamins/Cambridge results](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/9781108992602/type/ELEMENT)

### Inferences
- A personal sentence list draws on a real strength: formulaic language makes up a large share of fluent speech, and chunks give instant usable phrases plus raw material for later analysis. The "self-narration" source also makes the content personally relevant and high-frequency for that learner.
- The evidence does NOT show that memorized sentences turn into flexible grammar on their own and quickly. For a beginner, a few hundred memorized sentences will give phrasebook-like competence. Recombining them for new meanings (changing tense, politeness level, particles) needs either a lot more input or some explicit pattern-noticing. This matters especially for Korean, with its agglutinative verb endings and speech levels.
- Risk: AI-translated self-narration may produce unnatural or over-literal sentences. A list of non-idiomatic "chunks" undermines the main benefit, which is learning what native speakers actually say.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Wray's work (e.g. *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon*, 2002) or direct experiments comparing sentence-level vs single-word learning for adult beginners.
- No study tested AI-generated personalized sentence lists specifically.
- The Myles studies involved school-age classroom learners, not adults. Adult evidence on chunk unpacking was not found in this session.

## Q2. Retrieval practice (testing effect) and spaced repetition: do spaced schedules beat massed review?

### Takeaway
This is well supported, and it is the strongest part of the method. Retrieval practice and spacing are among the most robust findings in learning science, including for foreign vocabulary. Spacing beats massed review, especially at delayed tests. Expanding schedules are not clearly better than evenly spaced ones.

### Cited Findings
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science* 319, 966, "The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning"): students learned 40 Swahili–English word pairs. Repeated testing produced much better long-term retention than repeated studying, and once an item could be recalled, further restudy gave little benefit while further testing did. — [Science supporting material (Purdue)](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_ScienceSupportingMaterial.pdf); [APA Monitor 2008](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2008/06/testing)
- A later reanalysis by Soderstrom & Kerr examined whether restudy only looked ineffective because of how items were spaced in the original design. — [Soderstrom & Kerr PDF (UCLA Bjork Lab)](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_kerr.pdf)
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58) rated practice testing and distributed practice as the only "high utility" techniques of the ten reviewed. Rereading, highlighting, summarization, the keyword mnemonic and imagery were rated "low utility." — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)
- Kim & Webb (2022, *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319, DOI 10.1111/lang.12479), meta-analysis of spaced practice in L2 learning:
  - Spacing had a medium-to-large effect.
  - Shorter and longer gaps performed similarly on immediate tests, but longer gaps were better on delayed tests.
  - Equal and expanding schedules were statistically equivalent.
  - Moderators included learning target, number of sessions and retention interval.
  - Sample size is reported inconsistently in secondary sources (37 studies vs 48 experiments / 98 effect sizes / N = 3,411), so check the article. — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001); [Peeref](https://peeref.com/works/24199363)
- Productive (recall) retrieval formats beat recognition formats for gaining productive knowledge of word form in Nakata's paired-associate research. — [Redalyc review PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3398/339829651005.pdf); [IRAL/De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- In Webb (2005, *SSLA*), productive learning tasks produced larger gains across more aspects of word knowledge than receptive tasks. Receptive tasks mainly built receptive meaning knowledge. — [Cambridge, SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-learning-the-effects-of-reading-and-writing-on-word-knowledge/DDF362AE7B13D1949B1CD591DA2F3414)

### Inferences
- Step 3 (see the English, say the target sentence aloud, check) is a textbook retrieval practice drill, and productive (L1 to L2) recall builds the productive knowledge needed for speaking. This is well supported, provided the reviews are spaced across days (e.g. an SRS app such as Anki) rather than crammed.
- Step 2's passive "listen on repeat" is closer to re-exposure/restudy, which Dunlosky rates as low-utility for retention. It still has value for sound familiarity, but it is not a substitute for retrieval.
- Fancy expanding intervals are not essential, per Kim & Webb. What matters most is spacing at all and longer gaps for long-term retention.

### Gaps
- I found no studies on retrieval of whole sentences (as opposed to word pairs) by adult beginners in distant languages. Generalizing from word-pair studies is a reasonable extrapolation, not a direct test.
- I could not retrieve Kang's or Nakata's SRS schedule studies in full.

## Q3. Shadowing for listening and pronunciation

### Takeaway
This is partly to moderately supported. Shadowing has fairly consistent small-study evidence for improving bottom-up listening (phoneme perception), especially for lower-proficiency learners. Evidence for pronunciation is thinner. Much of the base comes from one research group (Hamada) and Japanese EFL university students, mostly at intermediate level, not total beginners.

### Cited Findings
- Hamada (2016) studied 44 Japanese undergraduates over a month of shadowing lessons. They significantly improved on a 20-item partial dictation test, which was interpreted as better phoneme perception. — [JALT Journal 2025 article citing Hamada](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jj47.1-art1.pdf); [Hamada Shadowing PDF (TESL Ontario)](https://contact.teslontario.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Hamada-Shadowing.pdf)
- After training, low-proficiency learners approached the intermediate group's initial level on phoneme perception and listening comprehension, so lower-level learners gained most. — [JALT Journal 47.1](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jj47.1-art1.pdf)
- Hamada argues that shadowing's main role for listening is to improve phoneme perception (bottom-up processing) by keeping attention on sound rather than meaning. — [Hamada, RELC Journal 2018 "Shadowing: What is it? How to use it. Where will it go?"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380); [Routledge book review (2017 book *Teaching EFL Learners Shadowing for Listening*)](https://revistaseug.ugr.es/index.php/portalin/article/download/13709/11860)
- Hamada (2019, *Applied Linguistics Review* 10(2), 193–217) compared different types of shadowing training on Japanese learners' listening and speaking. — [reference list in Dergipark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3392195)
- Hamada's 2018 RELC review noted there was no summative review of shadowing at that point. — [SAGE, RELC Journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380)
- CAUTION: One reference list cites "Li, S. (2018). A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of shadowing..." in *Language Learning* 68(1), 195–223. I could NOT verify that this exists, and it conflicts with Hamada's 2018 statement that no summative review existed. Treat it as possibly erroneous or fabricated and do not cite it. — [Dergipark PDF reference list](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3392195)
- A claimed 2025 systematic review of 44 pronunciation-shadowing studies appears only in a commercial blog with no authors or journal named, so it is unverified. — [Migaku blog](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/shadowing-a-practitioners-guide-to-the-technique-in-2026)

### Inferences
- Shadowing is a reasonable add-on for a beginner's listening and prosody, but its main proven benefit is sound perception, not meaning comprehension or grammar.
- Shadowing works best on material the learner already understands. Hamada's line suggests learners can't shadow well when overloaded, which fits with the method's practice of shadowing one's own known sentences.
- Caveat for Korean: shadowing AI TTS audio means copying synthetic prosody. Modern TTS is generally good, but it is not equivalent to native recordings for intonation and natural connected-speech processes. No study tested TTS-based shadowing.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Kadota's work (the originator of shadowing research in Japan) or any verified meta-analysis.
- I found no research on shadowing for absolute beginners or for Korean as L2.

## Q4. Explicit grammar instruction vs implicit learning for adults: is "no grammar at all" supported?

### Takeaway
This is NOT supported. Meta-analyses consistently find that instruction works and that explicit instruction is at least as effective as implicit, often more so, including for complex features. "Throw away grammar books" contradicts the evidence. A defensible version would be: "don't make grammar study your main activity; use it as a light reference to understand patterns in your sentences."

### Cited Findings
- Norris & Ortega (2000) meta-analysis: explicit treatments averaged about d = 1.13 vs about d = 0.54 for implicit treatments, a large vs medium effect favoring explicit instruction. Exact study and treatment counts vary across secondary sources, so verify them. — [Issuu (Implicit and explicit learning chapter)](https://issuu.com/betifca/docs/libro_ingles/33); [DeepDyve abstract](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/wiley/does-type-of-instruction-make-a-difference-substantive-findings-from-a-9dzVAhpcHv/)
- Methodological critique of Norris & Ortega: a re-examination argued that data quality, coding and effect-size issues undermine some of its conclusions. — [Columbia SALT, "Another Look at Norris and Ortega (2000)"](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434)
- Spada & Tomita (2010, *Language Learning*) analyzed 41 studies, split target features into simple and complex, and found larger effects for explicit over implicit instruction for BOTH types. — [University of Toronto record](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)
- Kang, Sok & Han (2019, *Language Teaching Research* 23(4), 428–453) covered 35 years (1980–2015) of form-focused instruction. The overall instruction effect was large (g = 1.06, 95% CI 0.84–1.29). Only a "minor difference" was found between explicit and implicit instruction. Significant moderators were outcome-measure type, learners' starting proficiency, setting and intensity. — [Switchboard evidence summary](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/thirty-five-years-of-isla-on-form-focused-instruction-a-meta-analysis/); [Figshare supplement](https://figshare.com/collections/Thirty-five_years_of_ISLA_on_form-focused_instruction_A_meta-analysis/4163639)
- A replication study notes that instruction-vs-no-instruction meta-analyses like Kang et al. cannot by themselves establish that one instruction type beats another. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1293322.pdf)
- Maeng (2020) meta-analyzed 40 studies in Korean EFL classrooms and found a positive comparative effect of explicit over implicit instruction. — [KoreaScholar / earticle record](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/370416)

### Inferences
- The best-supported reading: explicit grammar is clearly not harmful, and it is usually helpful for adults. Kang et al. suggest the explicit/implicit gap may be smaller than Norris & Ortega found, but nowhere does "zero grammar" come out ahead.
- Known caveat (not resolved here): many studies use tests that favor explicit knowledge (e.g. grammaticality judgments), which may inflate explicit effects relative to spontaneous speaking.
- For Korean, a small amount of explicit knowledge (Hangul, particles 은/는/이/가/을/를, verb-ending and politeness system) likely speeds up the "unpacking" of memorized sentences discussed in Q1.

### Gaps
- I did not find meta-analytic evidence specific to total beginners learning typologically distant languages.

## Q5. Passive background listening; pre-reading a transcript/glossing; lexical coverage thresholds

### Takeaway
"Passive immersion barely works" for beginners is partly true. Input that is mostly incomprehensible provides little learnable material, and coverage research shows learners need to know most of the words to comprehend. "Pre-study the transcript" is plausible and consistent with pre-teaching vocabulary research, but there is little direct evidence for transcript pre-reading specifically, and some researchers think it may short-circuit genuine listening practice.

### Cited Findings
- Hu & Nation (2000): 98% lexical coverage was needed for adequate unassisted reading comprehension of an easy fiction text. — [Leeds/White Rose (Dang & Webb) PDF citing Hu & Nation](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/135476/7/Dang%20%20%20Webb%20fourth%20submission.pdf); [Schmitt, Cobb et al. 2015 PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_cobb_etal_2015.pdf)
- van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013, *Applied Linguistics* 34(4)): 95% coverage allowed adequate listening comprehension for most learners, which implies about 2,000–3,000 word families. Nation's (2006) 98% estimate implies about 6,000–7,000 families. — [Cambridge replication article in Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE); [Scribd copy of van Zeeland & Schmitt](https://www.scribd.com/document/684149066/Van-Zeeland-2012)
- The listening threshold is contested: Stæhr (2009) found 98% coverage was needed for academic listening. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.831684/pdf)
- Pre-teaching vocabulary before listening helped beginning EFL learners' comprehension more than schema activation in a Japanese high-school study. Other sources report vocabulary pre-teaching helped higher-level learners but not lower-level ones, so the evidence is mixed. Most studies are small quasi-experiments. — [Yamanashi repository PDF](https://yamanashi.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1516/files/10_138-148.pdf); [IKIU Persian learner study](https://jtpsol.journals.ikiu.ac.ir/article_2820.html?lang=en)
- Osada (2001, as cited in Tangkakarn & Gampper 2020) argued that giving written text before listening is an inefficient way to practice listening. The 2020 study tested a "listen first, then read while listening" sequence, but I did not retrieve its results. — [IJI 2020 PDF](https://www.e-iji.net/dosyalar/iji_2020_3_53.pdf)
- Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis holds that conscious noticing is necessary to convert input into intake, which challenges Krashen's view that acquisition happens without conscious attention. Schmidt's own work has methodological critiques. — [Akita Univ. repository PDF](https://air.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2170/files/kougaku34%2829%29.pdf); [Thai journal PDF](https://so17.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JEL/article/download/116/83/310)

### Inferences
- A total beginner in Korean knows nearly 0% of the words in native content, far below the 95–98% thresholds. So passive background listening to native media is unlikely to drive much learning early on, which supports the video's skepticism. Listening to one's OWN known sentences on repeat is different: that input is near 100% comprehensible.
- Pre-studying a transcript is a sensible way to raise coverage before native content. The best-supported sequence is probably: listen first, study the transcript and unknown words, then re-listen several times. This keeps genuine listening practice and adds comprehension.
- Graded or learner-level input (beginner podcasts, learner YouTube with subtitles) is an alternative way to reach high coverage, and the method does not mention it.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous adult study isolating "passive background listening while doing other tasks" as a learning condition. The "barely works" claim rests on coverage logic and noticing theory, not on direct experiments found here.
- I did not retrieve glossing or input-enhancement meta-analyses. A visual input-enhancement meta-analysis exists in *SSLA*, but I didn't read it. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/visual-input-enhancement-and-grammar-learning-a-metaanalytic-review/B9D0C50B09928C20C94548B37B29A042)
- Lexical coverage research is mostly on English as L2. Thresholds for Korean (agglutinative, so different word-family counting) are uncertain.

## Q6. Sleep and memory consolidation: does reviewing before sleep improve retention?

### Takeaway
This is partly true and overstated. Good evidence shows that sleep between learning sessions benefits foreign vocabulary retention and relearning. The specific "review right before bed dramatically improves retention" framing goes beyond the evidence, which is based on small lab studies with word pairs.

### Cited Findings
- Mazza et al. (2016, *Psychological Science*, DOI 10.1177/0956797616659930) had 40 participants learn foreign vocabulary word pairs in two sessions 12 hours apart, with sleep between sessions vs a wake group.
  - The sleep group needed roughly half the relearning trials (about 3 vs about 6 to recall all 16 words).
  - At 1 week, the sleep group recalled about 15 pairs vs about 11 for the wake group, with benefits still observed at 6 months.
  - Differences were not explained by sleep quality, sleepiness or memory capacity. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/sleep-makes-relearning-faster-and-longer-lasting.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/08/160822083446.htm); [PubMed/BVS record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/bvsiec/resource/pt/mdl-27530500)
- A replication with a less demanding relearning criterion was registered. Its data were still being collected at the time of the record, and I don't know the outcome. — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02487151)

### Inferences
- The defensible advice: study, sleep, and review again the next morning. Spacing across a night of sleep is beneficial and is easy to build into a routine.
- Mazza's design confounds "sleep" with "spacing across a night." It supports evening study then morning relearning, not specifically "the last thing before sleep," and the effect was in a lab, on 16 word pairs.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Gaskell's lexical consolidation work (e.g. Dumay & Gaskell 2007) in this session.
- The replication's outcome is unknown.
- No evidence was found on sentence-level material or real-world learners.

## Q7. "Conversational in 6 weeks", realistic timelines, dropout/failure rates and Duolingo effectiveness

### Takeaway
"Conversational in 6 weeks" for a distant language like Korean is hype. FSI estimates about 2,200 class hours (88 weeks of full-time intensive study) for professional working proficiency, and nothing found supports basic conversation in 6 weeks of part-time study. "90% never reach basic conversation" has no traceable source. "Duolingo is a waste of time" is too strong: Duolingo's own studies show measurable reading and listening gains, but they are company-funded and say little about speaking.

### Cited Findings
- The US Foreign Service Institute places Korean in its hardest group for English speakers: about 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours, vs about 24–30 weeks (about 600–750 hours) for Category I languages like Spanish and French. Sources label the hardest group "Category IV" or "Category V" inconsistently. — [Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/nation/2023/11/113_240261.html); [Linguasphere](https://linguasphere.info/guides/learning/how-long-to-learn-a-language-english-speaker/); [Atlas & Boots](https://www.atlasandboots.com/?p=83528)
- The FSI figure is an average for a specific type of learner (intensive classes, trained diplomats, usually with prior language-learning experience) aiming at professional working proficiency, not basic conversation. — [Linguasphere](https://linguasphere.info/guides/learning/how-long-to-learn-a-language-english-speaker/); [FluentU](https://www.fluentu.com/blog/korean/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-korean/)
- Jiang et al. (2021, *Foreign Language Annals* 54) studied Duolingo Spanish and French learners who completed beginner-level units. Their reading and listening scores were comparable to US university students at the end of their 4th semester. A later Duolingo report claimed intermediate-level learners were comparable to 5th-semester students. — [NAU experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/evaluating-the-reading-and-listening-outcomes-of-beginning-level-/); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-teaches-5-semesters); [Duolingo whitepaper PDF](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/Duolingo_whitepaper_language_read_listen_2021.pdf)
- A *CALL-EJ* critique says the claim that participants used Duolingo as their "only learning tool" is not supported by the methods, since participants weren't screened for other learning. The research is company-funded, the sample is self-selected course completers, and outcomes were reading and listening rather than speaking. — [CALL-EJ PDF](https://callej.org/index.php/journal/article/download/419/347/1013)
- No official Duolingo completion rate exists. Duolingo says courses aren't "finished." Informal estimates (e.g. 0.01%–0.24% completion) have no reliable methodology. — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/duolingo-this-is-what-you-are-missing-7ba66559355a); [FAQ site](https://calendar-uk.co.uk/frequently-asked-questions/what-percentage-of-people-finish-a-duolingo-course)
- A 2018 study of adult online Dutch courses found dropout to be highly "front-loaded," with retention increasing strongly after the first module. — [Research Portal Belgium](https://www.researchportal.be/nl/node/4728782)

### Inferences
- If "conversational" means a few dozen rehearsed exchanges about one's own daily routine, a dedicated beginner with a personal sentence list could plausibly manage that in about 6 weeks. That is rehearsed phrase use, not open conversation. Understanding a native speaker's unpredictable replies at natural speed requires vocabulary and listening skills that coverage research (Q5) shows take far longer.
- High attrition is real but the "90%" figure appears unsourced. The front-loaded dropout finding suggests the biggest practical risk is quitting early, so a method that feels personal and motivating may help adherence even if its claims are inflated.
- Duolingo: the fair summary is "a low-intensity tool with modest, measurable receptive gains and weak speaking development," not "waste of time." It also shares the method's best features (retrieval and spacing) in gamified form.

### Gaps
- I found no source for the "90% of learners never reach basic conversation" statistic. Treat it as unverified.
- I found no independent (non-Duolingo-funded) RCT of Duolingo. No data on Duolingo Korean specifically.
- I found no empirical studies on hours to basic (A2) conversational ability in Korean for self-study adults. ACTFL/CEFR hour guides were not retrieved.

## Q8. Criticisms of output-heavy L1 to L2 recall drills and the role of comprehensible input

### Takeaway
This is a mixed or balanced picture. Comprehensible input is widely accepted as necessary, but Krashen's strong claim that input alone is sufficient has been challenged by Swain (output) and Schmidt (noticing). Output and productive retrieval have real support (Q2), but a method that is mostly L1 to L2 translation recall of a small self-generated list is likely input-poor. Its main risks are limited exposure, unnatural L2 from translation, and weak listening for real speech.

### Cited Findings
- Swain's Output Hypothesis came from her Canadian immersion studies, where students with years of comprehensible input still had weak productive accuracy. She argued that comprehensible output is also needed. — [Akita Univ. repository](https://air.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2170/files/kougaku34%2829%29.pdf); [Thai journal PDF](https://so17.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JEL/article/download/116/83/310)
- Swain proposed three functions of output: noticing, hypothesis formulation and testing, and metalinguistic reflection. Empirical support for output-triggered noticing is mixed: Izumi and colleagues did not find the predicted greater noticing of target forms. — [Dergipark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/1187987); [Serve repo PDF](https://serve.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/393/files/3338.pdf)
- Critics argue Swain overstates output, since many learners reach high proficiency mainly through input. Some scholars treat input and output as complementary. — [Thai journal PDF](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/hbds/article/download/180603/128178/517365)
- Productive (L1 to L2) recall builds productive knowledge better than receptive tasks, so for speaking it is well matched (Q2). — [Cambridge, Webb 2005 SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-learning-the-effects-of-reading-and-writing-on-word-knowledge/DDF362AE7B13D1949B1CD591DA2F3414)
- Receptive vocabulary size is consistently larger than productive size (Webb 2008), suggesting comprehension develops ahead of production. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-sizes-of-l2-learners/603A8FD24DBAA2874163DD36D8BAACA3)

### Inferences
- The method's L1 to L2 recall is well-designed for speaking practice. Its weakness is breadth: a personal list of a few hundred sentences provides a narrow input base. Adding lots of graded comprehensible input (beginner podcasts, learner materials, then transcript-supported native content) would address the input side.
- Using English prompts each time keeps the learner's mental routing through L1. Some teachers recommend also using L2-only cues (pictures, audio prompts) as proficiency grows. This is my inference; no study found here tested it.
